## 1. Objective

To facilitate understanding of the AI Regulation (EU AI Act) from a practical approach, providing hypothetical examples of high-risk AI systems that are used as cross-cutting cases across all the sandbox's technical guides.

**Target audience:** Companies and individuals seeking to understand the AI Regulation and consult the technical guides.

## 3. Key definitions of the Regulation (Art. 3 AI Reg.) with examples

### Fundamental concepts

- **AI system:** A machine-based system with varying levels of autonomy, that infers from input data to generate predictions, recommendations, or decisions that influence physical or virtual environments.
- **Placing on the market:** The first making available of an AI system on the Union market.
- **Making available on the market:** The supply of an AI system for distribution or use on the Union market (paid or free of charge).
- **Intended purpose:** The use for which the provider designs the system, according to its instructions, technical documentation, and sales materials.
- **Safety component:** A component whose failure or malfunction endangers the health or safety of persons or property.
- **Substantial modification:** A change not foreseen in the initial conformity assessment that affects compliance with requirements or the intended purpose. Triggers a new conformity assessment.

### Data in the AI lifecycle

- **Training data:** Data used to adjust the trainable parameters of the model.
- **Validation data:** Data used to evaluate the model and adjust hyperparameters (avoid underfitting/overfitting).
- **Testing data:** Independent data for final evaluation before placing on the market.
- **Input data:** Data provided to the system in production to generate outputs.

### Biometrics

- **Biometric identification:** Automated recognition of human characteristics to determine identity by comparing against a database (one-to-many).
- **Biometric verification:** One-to-one authentication comparing biometric data with previously provided data.
- **Biometric categorisation system:** AI that classifies persons into categories based on biometric data.
- **Remote biometric identification:** Identification at a distance, without active participation, comparing against a reference database.

### Other concepts

- **Serious incident:** An incident or defect that causes: death or serious harm to health; serious disruption of critical infrastructure; breach of fundamental rights; serious damage to property or the environment.
- **Real-world testing plan:** A document describing the objectives, methodology, scope, and organization of real-world tests.
- **Deep fake:** Image, audio, or video content generated by AI that simulates real persons, places, or events in a deceptive manner.
- **General-purpose AI model (GPAI):** A model trained at large scale with self-supervision, capable of performing multiple diverse tasks. Threshold: computational cost > 10^23 FLOPs.
- **Systemic risk of GPAI:** Models with high-impact capabilities that can affect public health, safety, or fundamental rights at scale. Threshold: > 10^25 FLOPs.
- **AI system lifecycle:** From initial requirements and design through deployment, operation, monitoring, and decommissioning.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Risk classification:** Determine whether the system falls under the categories of Annex I (sectoral legislation, e.g., medical devices) or Annex III (biometrics, employment, public services, law enforcement), as this dictates all obligations.

2. **Documented intended purpose:** Clearly define and document the intended purpose; any change of purpose may constitute a substantial modification requiring a new conformity assessment.

3. **Data governance throughout the lifecycle:** Separate and govern training, validation, testing, and input data; each type has distinct requirements for quality, representativeness, and traceability.

4. **Continuous post-market surveillance:** Providers must actively collect and examine usage experience after placing on the market to detect the need for corrective measures.

5. **Clear chain of responsibility:** Provider and deployer have differentiated obligations; both must notify incidents and maintain records.

6. **Safety components:** Identify whether the AI system acts as a safety component; if a failure could affect health/safety, obligations are stricter (e.g., insulin pump).

7. **Serious incidents:** Establish processes to detect, classify, and notify serious incidents (death, harm to health, critical infrastructure, fundamental rights, environment).

8. **Compliance with harmonised standards:** Where they exist, follow common specifications or justify equivalent technical solutions.

9. **Substantial modifications:** Any post-market change affecting the assessed conformity or the intended purpose requires re-assessment. Continuously monitor whether model updates or changes in the context of use constitute substantial modifications.

10. **Deep fakes and GPAI:** If general-purpose models are used, consider additional obligations (transparency of use, systemic risk if FLOP thresholds are exceeded).
# Guide 3. Conformity Assessment

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Reference standards:** ISO/IEC 17000:2004, ISO/IEC 17007:2009, prEN XXX AI Conformity Assessment Framework
**Illustrative use cases:** Smart insulin pump, Biometric attendance control

---

## 1. Concept of conformity assessment

**Conformity assessment** (Art. 3.20 AI Reg.): a process by which it is demonstrated whether a high-risk AI system meets the requirements of Chapter III, Section 2 of the Regulation.

- It is the **provider's responsibility**, even if design or production is subcontracted.
- It must be carried out **before placing on the market or putting into service**.
- Central article: **Art. 43** + Annexes VI and VII.

---

## 2. Two forms of conformity assessment

| Modality | Legal basis | Who assesses | What is examined |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internal control** (self-assessment) | Annex VI | The provider itself | QMS (Art. 17) + technical documentation + consistency of the design/development process with the technical documentation |
| **Notified body** | Annex VII | External notified body | QMS (Art. 17) + technical documentation (Annex IV) + ongoing surveillance of the approved QMS |

### 2.1 Internal control (Annex VI) — 3 steps

1. **Verify the quality management system (QMS):** Check that it complies with the 13 subsections of Art. 17.
2. **Verify the technical documentation:** Confirm that it covers the essential requirements of Arts. 8-15 of the Regulation. Assess conformity through documentation, tests, and trials.
3. **Verify consistency of the design/development process and post-market surveillance** with the technical documentation.

The entire process is carried out internally, must be fully documented, and must be available to the market surveillance authority.

### 2.2 Notified body (Annex VII) — Detailed process

**QMS analysis:**
- The provider submits a formal application including: provider data, list of AI systems, technical documentation for each system, QMS documentation (Art. 17), maintenance procedures, exclusivity declaration.
- The notified body evaluates and issues a reasoned decision (approval or not).
- Ongoing surveillance: periodic audits, unannounced on-site audits, recertification every 5 years.

**Technical documentation analysis:**
- Examination of the documentation (Para. 4.3, Annex VII).
- The body may access training, validation, and testing data (via API or other tools).
- It may require additional tests or justifications (Para. 4.4).
- As a last resort, it may access the training model and the trained model with its parameters (Para. 4.5), subject to IP protection and trade secrets.

**Three possible decision outcomes:**
- a) **Compliant** → a EU technical documentation assessment certificate is issued.
- b) **Non-compliant** → no certificate is issued.
- c) **Non-compliant with training data requirements** → no certificate is issued + specific considerations on data quality and reasons for non-compliance. Requires retraining before a new application.

---

## 3. Form of assessment depending on the type of AI system

### 3.1 Remote biometric identification systems (Annex III, point 1)

- **If the provider has applied harmonised standards or common specifications:** May choose between internal control OR notified body.
- **If harmonised standards have NOT been applied:** Notified body is mandatory.

**Competent notified bodies for biometrics:**
- General rule: the provider chooses the notified body.
- Exceptions:
  - If put into service by law enforcement, immigration, or asylum authorities → the notified body shall be the market surveillance authority.
  - If used by EU institutions/bodies → it shall be the European Data Protection Supervisor.

### 3.2 Other high-risk AI systems (Annex III, points 2-8)

- Assessed through the provider's **internal control**.
- Covered sectors: critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, justice, migration, democratic processes.

### 3.3 AI systems as products/safety components (Annex I, Section A)

- The assessment follows the **conformity assessment procedure of the corresponding product harmonisation legislation**.
- The AI Regulation requirements are integrated into that assessment.
- Key conclusions:
  - The assessment of AI Regulation requirements is carried out within the conformity assessment structure of the applicable product.
  - It cannot affect the overall product conformity assessment structure.

**Table of harmonisation legislation and their assessment:**

| Legislation | Conformity assessment |
|---|---|
| Directive 2006/42/EC — Machinery | Depends on the product: notified body or internal |
| Directive 2009/48/EC — Toys | Notified body if harmonised standards are not met |
| Directive 2014/33/EU — Lifts | Notified body **mandatory in all cases** |
| Regulation 2017/745 — Medical devices | Depends on the product |
| Regulation 2017/746 — In vitro diagnostics | Depends on the product |
| Regulation 2023/1230 — Machinery (new) | Notified body for AI with machine learning in safety functions |

**Current situation:** Since no harmonised standards or common specifications exist for AI Regulation requirements, **the notified body assessment cannot be avoided** even if the product legislation would otherwise allow it.

---

## 4. Substantial modification and re-assessment

A **new conformity assessment** is required (Art. 43.4) when there is a substantial modification:
- A change that affects compliance with the Regulation (e.g., change of OS, software architecture) not foreseen or planned.
- A change in the system's intended purpose.

**Exception for systems that continue to learn:** If post-market changes were foreseen by the provider and included in the initial technical documentation, they are NOT considered substantial modifications.

---

## 5. Conformity instruments

### 5.1 Harmonised standards (Art. 40)
- Adopted by CEN, CENELEC, or ETSI at the request of the Commission (timeframe ~3 years).
- Not mandatory, but their application confers a **presumption of conformity**.
- **Currently no harmonised standards exist** for the AI Regulation.

### 5.2 Common technical specifications (Art. 41)
- Adopted by the European Commission when harmonised standards are absent or insufficient.
- Not mandatory, but they confer a presumption of conformity.
- If not applied, the provider must justify equivalent technical solutions.
- **Currently none exist** for the AI Regulation.

### 5.3 Other technical specifications
- National or international standards (ISO, IEC, etc.).
- **Do not confer a presumption of conformity**, but serve as evidence.
- The provider must demonstrate that compliance with the requirements is achieved through them.

### 5.4 Specific presumptions of conformity (Art. 42)
- **Data:** If the system is trained and tested with data reflecting the specific environment of use → it is presumed compliant with Art. 10.4 (data governance).
- **Cybersecurity:** If the system holds a certificate or declaration of conformity from a cybersecurity scheme published in the OJEU → it is presumed compliant with Art. 15.

### 5.5 EU declaration of conformity (Art. 47)
- Mandatory document signed by the provider declaring conformity.
- In writing or with electronic signature.
- Retain for 10 years after placing on the market.
- Content as per Annex V, translated into the language of the Member State of marketing.
- **Does not apply to general-purpose AI models (GPAI).**

### 5.6 CE marking (Art. 48)
- Visible, legible, and indelible indicator of conformity.
- For digital systems: digital CE marking accessible from the interface.
- Followed by the identification number of the notified body.
- **Does not apply to general-purpose AI models.**

---

## 6. Notifying authorities and notified bodies

### Notifying authorities
- Public authority of each Member State responsible for assessing, designating, and notifying conformity assessment bodies.
- In Spain: one notifying authority per harmonisation law. ENAC supervises.
- According to the Spanish AI draft bill: the State Secretariat for Digitalization shall be the Notifying Authority.

### Notified bodies
- Bodies officially designated to carry out conformity assessments.
- May be public or private entities.
- Regulated in Arts. 29-39 of the AI Regulation.
- **Currently no notified body exists** for the purposes of the AI Regulation.
- In Spain: National Certification Centre for Medical Devices (the only one for medical devices).

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Determine the conformity assessment pathway early:** The type of AI system (Annex I vs Annex III, biometrics vs others) determines whether internal control or a notified body is required. This decision affects timelines and costs from the design phase.

2. **Integrate conformity from conception:** The evidence needed for the assessment must be integrated from the earliest product design phases, not added retroactively.

3. **Technical documentation as the centrepiece:** It is the primary support for evidencing compliance. It must be clear, organised according to Annex IV, and cover Arts. 8-15. Sandbox Guide 15 is the reference.

4. **Proportionate but rigorous QMS:** QMS requirements are proportionate to the size of the organisation (a benefit for SMEs), but the required level of protection is not reduced. Cover the 13 subsections of Art. 17.

5. **Ongoing QMS surveillance:** After initial approval, the QMS must remain active, with periodic audits, recertification every 5 years, and communication of changes to the notified body.

6. **Monitor substantial modifications:** Any change not foreseen in the initial technical documentation (change of OS, architecture, purpose) requires a new assessment. For systems that continue to learn, anticipate expected changes in the initial documentation to avoid re-assessments.

7. **Access to data and models by the notified body:** Be prepared to provide access (via API or other means) to training, validation, and testing datasets, and potentially to the trained model. IP protection is respected.

8. **Current absence of harmonised standards:** To date, no harmonised standards or common specifications exist for the AI Regulation, meaning that for remote biometrics without standards, the notified body is mandatory. Other technical specifications (ISO, IEC) may be used as supplementary evidence.

9. **Participation in sandboxes as an advantage:** The documentation generated in regulatory sandboxes can be used as evidence of conformity. Both authorities and notified bodies will view this positively.

10. **Realistic timelines:** In Spain's medical device sector, the average conformity assessment time with a notified body ranges from 6 to 18 months. Plan with adequate margin.

11. **EU declaration of conformity + CE marking:** Mandatory for high-risk systems (not for GPAI). Retain the declaration for 10 years. The digital CE marking must be accessible from the system's interface.

12. **SMEs and startups:** The European Commission will develop a simplified technical documentation form. Assessment fees will be reduced proportionally to the size of the company.
# Guide 4. Quality Management System

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Technical standard under development:** prEN 18286 "Artificial Intelligence — Quality Management System for EU AI Act Regulatory Purposes"
**Central article:** Art. 17 AI Reg. (13 subsections)
**Illustrative use cases:** Cross-cutting fictitious company analysed subsection by subsection

---

## 1. Concept of quality management system (QMS)

A QMS, according to ISO 9001, is a set of processes for the continuous improvement of an organisation's performance. In the context of the AI Regulation, its purpose is to guarantee **the health, safety, and fundamental rights** of persons in the use of AI systems.

- **Mandatory for providers** of high-risk AI systems (Art. 17).
- Must be documented through **policies, procedures, and written instructions**.
- No specific format is prescribed (internal policies, technical procedures, annexes, etc.).
- Many of its elements are developed in detail in other sandbox guides (risks, data, cybersecurity, accuracy, robustness, etc.).

---

## 3. Detail of each element

### 3.1 Regulatory compliance, conformity assessment, and change management (17.1.a)

- The QMS must indicate the **applicable conformity assessment procedure** (internal self-assessment or external control by a notified body).
- **Change management:** Maintain a list of software/firmware, libraries, and third-party components with a complete version history.
- Distinguish between **substantial changes** (may require a new conformity assessment), **non-substantial changes**, and **predefined changes** (foreseen in the product lifecycle).
- Each change must be subject to the same quality controls as the complete system.

### 3.2 Design, design control, and design verification (17.1.b)

- The provider is responsible for the design process and its systematic verification.
- The design must be based on technical/functional requirements and on mitigation measures from the risk management plan.
- **Three key aspects integrated into the design:**
  - **Cybersecurity:** Asset inventory, security controls from the design phase, involvement of the DPO (without operational cybersecurity responsibilities), technical dashboard.
  - **Accuracy:** Metrics appropriate to the intended purpose, verification of the objective function, mechanisms to ensure accuracy throughout the lifecycle.
  - **Robustness:** Properties of reliability, stability, sensitivity, relevance, and achievability. Metrics aligned with the risk plan and the intended purpose.

### 3.3 Development, quality control, and quality assurance (17.1.c)

- Tracking of design specifications using **ML-OPS** tools (open source or commercial).
- Traceability from design to implementation throughout development.
- **Cybersecurity in development:** Implement security controls against vulnerabilities, adversarial attacks, and exploitation of flaws. Proposed actions: red/blue team, specific testing with automated tests, continuous SAST and DAST, CVE/CWE analysis, generic and AI-specific penetration testing.
- **Accuracy in development:** Automated tests at each stage, data preprocessing, avoidance of overfitting, use of baseline models for comparison.
- **Robustness in development:** Verification (design requirements are met) + Validation (intended purpose requirements are met). Additional parameters: efficiency and performance.

### 3.4 Examination, testing, and validation (17.1.d)

- Tests **before, during, and after** development, at a defined frequency.
- Scope proportionate to the risk and design of the system (Art. 17.2).
- Record at minimum: data and model version, expected vs. obtained results, whether the test validates the assessed aspect, criticality in case of failure.
- **Accuracy:** Statistical significance tests and data/model benchmarks (update them as they evolve).
- **Robustness:** Systematic experimentation: plan, conduct, analyse, interpret, decide; document evidence.
- **Cybersecurity:** Cybersecurity is in itself a continuous process of examination and testing.

### 3.5 Technical specifications, standards, and harmonised standards (17.1.e)

- The QMS must document: a list of **harmonised standards** applied, a list of **common specifications** applied, and **technical specifications** (national or international) with the requirements they cover.
- **Currently no harmonised standards or common specifications exist** for the AI Regulation.
- In the meantime, the QMS must contain the sandbox's technical guides and the specifications based on national/international standards that the provider has applied.

### 3.6 Data management systems and procedures (17.1.f)

- Cover: collection, analysis, labelling, storage, filtering, mining, aggregation, and retention of data.
- Data governance practices: appropriate design, collection processes, processing operations (annotation, cleaning, enrichment, aggregation), formulation of assumptions, assessment of availability/quantity/adequacy, **bias analysis**, detection and remediation of gaps or deficiencies.
- Data must be relevant, representative, complete, with appropriate statistical properties, and consider the geographical/behavioural/functional context.
- Without prejudice to the GDPR (data minimisation, purpose limitation, impact assessments).
- Special categories of personal data may only be processed when **strictly necessary** for the surveillance, detection, and correction of biases.

### 3.7 Risk management system (17.1.g)

- Objective: **identification and analysis of risks + implementation of mitigation measures**.
- Risk = probability x impact.
- Special attention to risks affecting **health, safety, and fundamental rights**.
- Developed in detail in the Risk Management Guide (Guide 5).

### 3.8 Post-market surveillance system (17.1.h)

- A set of processes and tools for collecting activity indicators from the AI system **after placing on the market**.
- Assess compliance with the requirements of Ch. III, Section 2 throughout the entire lifecycle.
- Developed in the Post-Market Surveillance Guide (Guide 13).

### 3.9 Serious incident notification (17.1.i)

- Document: chain of responsibility (including DPO), evidence recording and preservation mechanism, incident resolution chain, notification timelines for authorities.
- The deployer must inform the provider without delay through the established channel.
- If the incident affects multiple deployers: provide clear procedural instructions.
- Developed in the Incident Management Guide (Guide 14).

### 3.10 Communication (17.1.j)

- Establish communication procedures with: competent national authorities (including sectoral ones), notified bodies, other operators, clients, and stakeholders.
- Document: communication channels, document formats, responsible persons, tools for accessing data/logs/source code.
- Access to source code or data only when required under the applicable regulatory procedure.

### 3.11 Documentation (17.1.k)

- Maintain documentation throughout the **entire lifecycle** of the system (from design to decommissioning) and for the periods required by the Regulation after decommissioning.
- Establish: change tracking procedure, chain of responsibility for updates, document management system (repositories, corporate quality management systems, or equivalents).
- Technical documentation serving as instructions for the deployer must be accessible to them.

### 3.12 Resource management (17.1.l)

Aligned with ISO 9001, four categories of resources:

| Resource | Focus |
|---|---|
| **People** | Profiles needed to address all elements of the QMS |
| **Infrastructure** | Software, hardware, information and communication technologies; testing infrastructure |
| **Operating environment** | Human, physical, social, psychological factors (temperature, lighting, stress prevention, etc.) |
| **Monitoring and measurement resources** | Continuous monitoring of technical parameters (accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity), organisational and human aspects, supervision of automated decisions |
| **Knowledge** | Experience, lessons learned, internal sources (IP, documentation) and external sources (standards, clients) |

### 3.13 Accountability framework (17.1.m)

Defines responsibilities of management and technical staff for all elements of the QMS.

**Proposed responsibility assignment table:**

| Requirement | Proposed responsible party |
|---|---|
| Regulatory compliance strategy | Legal profile |
| Design techniques | Design team |
| Development techniques | Development team |
| Examination, testing, and validation | Red/blue team or QA team |
| Technical specifications and standards | Legal profile + technical profile |
| Data management | CDO or AI system manager |
| Risk management | CRO or AI system manager |
| Post-market surveillance | QA team |
| Serious incident notification | Quality manager |
| Communication with NCA | Legal/compliance department + DPO |
| Documentation record-keeping | Compliance team |
| Resource management | HR |

## 4. Proportionality to the size of the organisation (Art. 17.2)

- The implementation of the QMS shall be **proportionate to the size of the provider's organisation**.
- **Does not reduce the level of protection** or the need to comply with all requirements.
- Allows adapting the **form of documenting and organising** the requirements to the size of the entity.
- **Recital 146:** Microenterprises may establish the QMS in a simplified manner, reducing administrative burden and costs. The Commission will develop specific guidelines for microenterprises.

---

## 5. Financial entities and providers subject to sectoral legislation

### Art. 17.3 — Providers with an existing sectoral QMS
- If already subject to QMS obligations under EU sectoral law, they may **integrate the 13 elements of Art. 17.1** into their existing quality management system.

### Art. 17.4 — Financial entities
- If they comply with internal governance requirements under EU financial services legislation, the QMS is **presumed to be fulfilled**, **except** for subsections g) (risk management), h) (post-market surveillance), and i) (incident notification), which must always be complied with.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **The QMS is mandatory and must be documented from the outset:** Every provider of high-risk AI systems must implement a QMS covering the 13 subsections of Art. 17, documented through policies, procedures, and written instructions. No specific format is prescribed.

2. **Integrate the 13 elements as a coherent system:** The elements are not independent; the QMS must function as an integrated whole. Risk management feeds into design, design defines testing, testing informs post-market surveillance, and so on.

3. **Every change requires the same quality controls as the complete system:** Quality does not apply only to initial development; any post-deployment modification must go through the same design, verification, validation, and documentation processes.

4. **Distinguish types of changes:** Substantial changes (require a new conformity assessment), non-substantial changes, and predefined changes (foreseen in the technical documentation). Document this classification in the QMS.

5. **Cybersecurity, accuracy, and robustness are addressed from the design phase:** These three aspects must be incorporated into the system design and maintained throughout the entire lifecycle, with defined metrics, controls, and specific testing procedures for each.

6. **Complete traceability through ML-OPS:** Use ML-OPS tools to trace from design specifications to implementation and testing. Retain evidence of all validations performed.

7. **Rigorous data governance compatible with the GDPR:** The QMS must document how collection, processing, bias analysis, and gap detection in data are implemented. Special categories of data are only used when strictly necessary.

8. **Assign clear responsibilities (accountability):** Each element of the QMS must have an identified responsible party. In SMEs these roles may be concentrated; in large companies they are distributed among specialised teams (legal, QA, CDO, CRO, HR, etc.).

9. **Proportionality does not reduce obligations, only their form:** Microenterprises and SMEs may simplify the form of documenting and implementing the QMS, but must comply with all requirements. The level of protection is not reduced.

10. **Financial entities: partial presumption of compliance:** If they already comply with internal governance requirements under EU financial legislation, they only need to additionally cover risk management (g), post-market surveillance (h), and incident notification (i).

11. **Absence of harmonised standards:** As long as they do not exist, the QMS must contain the sandbox's technical guides and the technical specifications based on national/international standards (ISO, IEC) that the provider has applied.

12. **Continuously monitor system parameters:** Monitoring and measurement resources must cover both technical parameters (accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity) and organisational and human aspects (supervision of automated decisions), especially for systems that continue to learn in production.
# Guide 5. Risk Management System

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Technical standards under development:** prEN 18228 "AI Risk Management", ISO/IEC 23894 "AI — Guidance on risk management"
**Central article:** Art. 9 AI Reg. (10 subsections)
**Illustrative use cases:** Employee promotion system, smart insulin pump

---

## 1. Concept of risk management system

A risk management system is a system whose objective is the **identification and analysis of risks** and the **implementation of mitigation measures**.

- **Risk** = probability x impact.
- Special focus on risks affecting **the health, safety, and fundamental rights** of persons.
- An **iterative and continuous** process spanning the entire AI system lifecycle (design, development, marketing, and post-market).
- Mandatory for providers of high-risk AI systems (Art. 9) and certain general-purpose AI systems.

### Risk ecosystem

AI systems → exposed to **threats** → which exploit **vulnerabilities** → generating **inherent risk** → mitigated with **controls** → result: **residual risk**.

Ways to treat risks: **mitigate** (preventive/detective controls), **accept**, **avoid** (discontinue the activity), or **transfer** (e.g., insurance policies).

---

## 2. Proportionality in risk management

- The measures are indicative and can be implemented proportionally to the risks, their impact, and the cost of implementation.
- Risks to **health, safety, and fundamental rights** must be given special consideration regardless of the size of the organisation.
- Each organisation will select the appropriate and necessary control measures.

## 4. Risk management system processes

The RMS is composed of sequential processes framed between **leadership and commitment** (above) and **monitoring and continuous improvement** (below):

1. Determination of risk appetite
2. AI system context
3. Risk identification
4. Risk analysis and assessment
5. Risk response
6. Documentation and communication

### 4.1 Determination of risk appetite

- **What it is:** The level of risk the organisation is willing to accept in relation to the impact on health, safety, and fundamental rights.
- **How to define it:** Quantitatively, by establishing a scale (e.g., 1 to 15) and selecting the maximum acceptable threshold.
- Example: a scale of 3 probability levels x 5 impact levels = maximum risk of 15. If the risk appetite is 4, any risk above that requires treatment measures.
- **Importance:** Without a defined risk appetite, it is not possible to assess whether identified risks are acceptable or to determine the necessary treatment measures.

### 4.2 AI system context

- **What it is:** The external and internal environment where the system is designed, developed, and used.
- Relevant elements: economic/regulatory factors, technological context, AI maturity within the organisation, data culture.
- The most important: those related to **health, safety, and fundamental rights** (e.g., right to non-discrimination, personal data protection).
- Inventory in a document (e.g., Excel) the contextual elements that impact the risk analysis, with special attention to the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights.

### 4.3 Risk identification

A process of discovering, recognising, and documenting risks. Three steps:

1. **Identify the components** of the AI system (see Annex B: actors, data, environments/tools, processes).
2. **Identify the threats** associated with those components through context analysis.
3. **Link threats with vulnerabilities** of components that may materialise into risks.

Example: Employee promotion system → component "training data" → threat "overrepresentation" → risk "discrimination in promotion".

- Only identified risks can be assessed and treated; unidentified risks remain outside the management system.

### 4.4 Risk analysis and assessment

- Determine for each risk: **impact** (scale e.g., 1-5: non-existent to critical) and **probability** (scale e.g., 1-3: unlikely to very likely).
- **Risk = impact x probability** (a qualitative and subjective exercise, on a coherent conceptual scale).
- Compare the resulting value with the **risk appetite** to decide whether the risk is acceptable or requires treatment.
- Example: risk of personal data disclosure → probability 3 x impact 4 = risk 12. With a risk appetite of 4, the threshold is exceeded → treatment measures are required.

### 4.5 Risk response

Four main treatment options:

| Option | Description |
|---|---|
| **Mitigate** | Implement controls to reduce probability or impact (e.g., RBAC, cybersecurity controls). Preferred option. |
| **Accept** | Accept the consequences of the risk (when it is below the risk appetite). |
| **Avoid** | Do not initiate or discontinue the activity generating the risk. |
| **Transfer** | Transfer the risk through contracts or insurance. |

Complete response process:
1. Determine the type of treatment measure.
2. Establish an **implementation plan** (e.g., Gantt chart, prioritisation by mitigation impact).
3. **Document and report residual risks** (consult the transparency guide).
4. Establish **review and monitoring periods** for the RMS.

### 4.6 Technical documentation of the RMS

- Follow the steps in section 4 and reflect them in a structured document.
- Special attention to the documentation of **residual risks**.
- An illustrative Excel document is attached ("Illustrative example of the development of a risk management system") with practical use cases.

### 4.7 Communication and consultation

- All involved actors must be aware of the RMS and collaborate on its completeness.
- Communication with internal and external stakeholders at all stages.
- Special attention to impacts on health, safety, and fundamental rights.

### 4.8 Monitoring and continuous improvement

- Establish review and update periods for the RMS.
- Review the risk inventory, the analysis/assessment of new and existing risks, and the mitigation measures.
- Monitoring must be carried out at each stage of the lifecycle.

### 4.9 Leadership and commitment

Senior management must:
- a) Publish a **risk management declaration or policy** (see Annex G).
- b) Ensure **sufficient resources** for risk management.
- c) **Assign authority, responsibility, and accountability** at the appropriate levels.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Define risk appetite from the outset:** Quantitatively establish the maximum acceptable risk level for health, safety, and fundamental rights. Without this threshold, it is not possible to assess whether identified risks require treatment or to dimension the necessary measures.

2. **The RMS is an iterative process, not a one-off exercise:** Art. 9 requires periodic reviews and updates throughout the entire lifecycle. Each stage (design, development, deployment, post-market) requires monitoring and updating of the risk inventory.

3. **Identify risks systematically by component:** Inventory the AI system's components (data, actors, tools, processes), then identify threats associated with each component through context analysis, and finally link threats with vulnerabilities. Unidentified risks remain outside the management system.

4. **Risk = impact x probability, compared with risk appetite:** The assessment is qualitative and subjective but must be coherent and documented. Risks exceeding the risk appetite require mandatory treatment measures.

5. **Four risk response options:** Mitigate (controls), accept, avoid, or transfer. Mitigation is the preferred option. Document the justification for each decision and plan implementation with clear priorities.

6. **Document residual risks explicitly:** After applying mitigation measures, residual risks must be documented and reported to system users in accordance with the transparency guide. The overall residual risk of the system must be considered acceptable.

7. **Special attention to fundamental rights:** The guide details 14 rights from the EU Charter that may be affected. The risk analysis must explicitly incorporate the assessment of impact on each relevant right, with documented real-world examples of violations.

8. **Protection of minors as a specific requirement:** Art. 9.9 requires assessing whether the system may affect persons under 18 and incorporating the resulting risks into the RMS, even if the system is not directly aimed at minors.

9. **Integrate the RMS with post-market surveillance:** Data collected by the post-market monitoring system must feed into the identification, analysis, and assessment process for new risks, closing the management cycle.

10. **Leadership and commitment from senior management:** Management must publish a risk management policy, ensure sufficient resources, and assign clear responsibilities. The AI RMS must be integrated with the organisation's overall risk management.

11. **Effectiveness indicators to measure controls:** Define concrete metrics for each risk management measure and for each implemented control (Annex E). These indicators allow assessing whether the measures are adequate and whether the system meets the requirements.

12. **Testing before marketing, planned from the design phase:** Tests must be designed based on parameters, metrics, and thresholds aligned with the intended purpose, executed during development, and completed before putting into service. Real-world condition testing requires revocable informed consent.
# Guide 6. Human Oversight

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Technical standard under development:** prEN 18229-1 "AI Trustworthiness Framework — Part 1: Logging, Transparency and Human Oversight"
**Core article:** Art. 14 AI Act (5 paragraphs)
**Illustrative use cases:** Granting financial aid to families without resources, Intelligent insulin pump

## 1. Concept of human oversight

Human oversight ensures that people can **make informed autonomous decisions** in relation to AI systems. The principle of human oversight **must occupy a central place in the system's functionality**.

- Oversight is exercised through **governance mechanisms**: human participation, human control, or human command.
- **Human participation:** Ability for human beings to intervene in all decision cycles of the system (not always possible or desirable).
- **Human control:** Intervention of human beings during the system's life cycle and in monitoring its operation.
- **Human command:** Ability to oversee the overall activity of the system and **decide how and when to use it in a given situation** (includes not using it, ignoring decisions, interrupting it).
- **Inverse principle:** The lower the level of oversight a person can exercise, the greater the verifications and governance required.
- The **ultimate responsibility** for the actions of the AI system lies with the people in the provider and deployer entities who are responsible for it.

### Relationship with other requirements

Human oversight is closely linked to:
- **Transparency** (Art. 13): To oversee a system, it is necessary to understand how it works, which requires it to be transparent.
- **Risk management** (Art. 9): For effective and comprehensive oversight, it is necessary to have control over the system and be able to manage risks.

## 3. Requirements of Art. 14

### 3.1 Design and development for effective oversight (Art. 14.1)

High-risk AI systems shall be **designed and developed** so that they can be effectively overseen by **natural persons** during the period they are in use, including appropriate **human-machine interface tools**.

### 3.2 Risks to fundamental rights (Art. 14.2)

The objective of human oversight is to **prevent or minimize risks** to health, safety, or fundamental rights, both in use in accordance with the intended purpose and in reasonably foreseeable misuse. Human oversight must integrate **risk management** in accordance with Art. 9.

### 3.3 Types of measures (Art. 14.3)

Oversight measures shall be **proportionate** to the risks, the level of autonomy, and the context of use:
- **14.3.a:** Measures that the provider defines and integrates when technically feasible, before placing on the market.
- **14.3.b:** Measures that the provider defines before introduction and that are **suitable for the deployer to put into practice**.

### 3.4 Understanding and Autonomy (Art. 14.4)

The persons in charge of human oversight must be able to:

| Sub-paragraph | Capability | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 14.4.a | **Understand** capabilities and limitations | Monitor operation, detect anomalies and unexpected behavior |
| 14.4.b | **Be aware** of automation bias | Avoid automatically or excessively trusting the system's results |
| 14.4.c | **Interpret** results correctly | Use available interpretation methods and tools |
| 14.4.d | **Decide** not to use the system | Discard, invalidate, or revert output results |
| 14.4.e | **Intervene or interrupt** the system | Press a stop button or similar procedure to halt safely |

### 3.5 Biometric identification (Art. 14.5)

For **remote biometric identification** systems (Annex III, point 1, letter a):
- The deployer **shall not take any decision** based on the identification generated by the system, **unless at least two persons** with competence, training, and authority have separately verified and confirmed said identification.
- **Exception:** The two-person verification does not apply to systems used for law enforcement, migration, border control, or asylum purposes, when EU or national law considers it to be disproportionate.

## 4. Applicable measures to achieve human oversight

### 4.1 Design and development measures for effective oversight (MV1)

- To oversee a system, it is necessary to **know how it is designed and built**.
- The design and construction dimensions are defined in the second chapter of the Regulation (Arts. 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15).
- The system must provide information to **all profiles** that interact with it, adapting the language to their level of communication.
- **User interface** that provides easily understandable information, visually and textually in natural language, to facilitate human oversight.

**Who applies it:**
- **Deployer:** Identifies the Regulation requirements that must be met and ensures through acceptance testing that the system supports them.
- **Provider:** Implements the technical measures aligned with said requirements.

### 4.2 Enabling a human-machine interface — HMI (MV2)

- Design and development measures must be **reflected in an interface** that allows monitoring and oversight of operation.
- Oversight must be feasible for both **technical and non-technical profiles**.
- High-risk AI systems have a recent deployment of HMI interfaces for monitoring and oversight, still in an embryonic phase.
- The **provider** supplies the interface; the **deployer** uses it while the system is in use.

### 4.3 Governance model (MV3)

The IT governance model is key to achieving human oversight. It must include:

- **Multidisciplinary organizational structure:** Business managers, technicians, legal professionals, auditors, and other profiles throughout the life cycle.
- **Procedures** that allow overseeing the solution from conceptualization and design **until it is in use**. For remote biometric identification systems: separate verification by two persons.
- **Risk management** as a fundamental part of the IT governance model, with periodic evaluations throughout the entire life cycle, especially when the system is in use.
- **Training plans** that equip all persons who interact with the system (design measures, interfaces, governance system).

**Who applies it:**
- The **provider** must have a governance model during construction and use, and provide guidelines to the deployer.
- The **deployer** shall adapt said governance system to the reality of its organizational structure and procedures.

### 4.4 Awareness: Forced error (MV4)

- The system may include a **forced error mode** that generates erroneous outputs in a controlled and deliberate manner.
- Objective: **Test the judgment and possible overconfidence** of the persons who will use the system in production and evaluate their oversight capability.
- Activatable only in **test environments** (not in production, so as not to induce real errors).
- The deployer will use it as a **training tool** before going to production.

### 4.5 Governance: Human in/on the loop (MV5)

Three levels of autonomy of the AI system:

| Level | Concept | Description | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human in Command** (HIC) | Human command | A human being is the **ultimate responsible party** and makes critical decisions in the system's operation | Global principle always present |
| **Human-in-the-loop** (HITL) | *Ex ante* oversight | Human intervention in **each action** of the system. The action is partially automated. A person validates each action | **Recommended for high risk** |
| **Human-on-the-loop** (HOTL) | *Ex post* oversight | Human intervention **after the fact**. The action is **fully automated**, but the possibility of reverting it is enabled | **Not recommended for high risk** due to the risk of hardly reversible impact |

- Regardless of the level, the system must have an **immediate deactivation mechanism**, without prejudice to a Business Continuity Plan.
- The system must **log both actions** (system proposal and final execution by the responsible person) following the Records guide (Art. 12).
- The **deployer** determines the level of autonomy; the **provider** implements the technical mechanisms for each level.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Human oversight is a design requirement, not an afterthought:** High-risk AI systems must be designed and developed from the outset so that natural persons can effectively oversee them, including appropriate human-machine interface tools (Art. 14.1).

2. **Inverse principle of oversight:** The lower the level of oversight a person can exercise over an AI system, the greater the verifications, controls, and governance required. This principle must guide design decisions.

3. **Five capabilities the system must guarantee to overseers:** Understanding capabilities and limitations (14.4.a), being aware of automation bias (14.4.b), correctly interpreting results (14.4.c), deciding not to use the system or reverting results (14.4.d), and intervening or interrupting the system safely (14.4.e).

4. **Human-in-the-loop (HITL) is the recommended level for high risk:** Ex ante human intervention (validating each action before executing it) is recommended for high-risk systems. The Human-on-the-loop (HOTL) level, where the action is fully automated and only reverted after the fact, is not recommended due to the risk of hardly reversible impact on fundamental rights.

5. **Always Human in Command (HIC) as a global principle:** Regardless of the autonomy level chosen (HITL or HOTL), a human being must always be the ultimate responsible party for the system's critical decisions.

6. **Mandatory immediate deactivation mechanism:** The system must have a stop button or equivalent procedure that allows its safe shutdown, along with a Business Continuity Plan to restore the functions of the supported process.

7. **Automation bias is an explicit risk:** People tend to over-rely on the output of an AI system, even more than on another person. The system must include measures to combat this bias, such as the forced error mode to test the judgment of overseers.

8. **Forced error as a training tool:** Including a mode that deliberately generates erroneous outputs in test environments allows evaluating the oversight capability of supervisors and training them to detect errors. Only activatable in test environments, never in production.

9. **Multidisciplinary governance model from design to use:** The model must include organizational structure (business, technical, legal, audit), oversight procedures, integrated risk management, and training plans for all persons who interact with the system.

10. **Differentiated responsibilities between provider and deployer:** The provider implements the technical measures and provides governance guidelines; the deployer adapts them to their organization, determines the level of autonomy, ensures compliance in production, and is responsible for continuous oversight.

11. **Remote biometric identification: dual human verification:** For these systems, no decision may be based on the identification generated by the system without at least two competent persons having separately verified and confirmed said identification. Exception limited to migration/asylum/border purposes if considered disproportionate.

12. **32 minimum human oversight documents:** The guide identifies specific documentation for each measure (design, HMI, governance, forced error, HITL/HOTL), covering both the provider's and deployer's obligations for each article of the Regulation.
# Guide 7. Data and data governance

## 1. Concept of data governance

Data governance in the context of AI is the set of elements (policies, procedures, processes, standards) implemented to ensure that the data used in training, validation, and testing are **adequate, relevant, sufficiently representative** and meet the established quality and completeness requirements.

- The lack of governance can lead to **biased or inaccurate** results, materializing as a risk to health, safety, or fundamental rights.
- Mandatory for providers of high-risk AI systems and certain general-purpose AI systems.
- Art. 10 is fundamentally oriented toward the **provider**; the deployer must apply the same measures when it controls the input data (Art. 26.4).

## 3. Requirements of Art. 10

### 3.1 Datasets (Art. 10.1-10.3)

High-risk AI systems that use model training techniques with data shall be developed from **training, validation, and test datasets** that meet quality criteria (paragraphs 2 to 5).

Dataset requirements:
- **Relevant and representative**, sufficiently complete, with a minimum of errors.
- **Adequate statistical properties** in relation to the persons or groups on whom the system will be used.
- Consider the **characteristics of the specific geographic, contextual, behavioral, or functional environment** of use.

### 3.2 Data governance and management practices (Art. 10.2)

The practices focus on:
- a) Adequate **design** decisions
- b) Data **collection processes** and data origin
- c) **Processing operations** for preparation: annotation, labeling, cleaning, updating, enrichment, aggregation
- d) **Formulation of assumptions** about what the data measure and represent
- e) **Prior assessment** of the availability, quantity, and adequacy of the datasets
- f) Examination of **possible biases** affecting health, safety, or fundamental rights
- g) **Measures** to detect, prevent, and mitigate biases
- h) Detection of **gaps or deficiencies** in the data and ways to address them

### 3.3 Special categories of personal data (Art. 10.5)

Providers may **exceptionally** process special categories of personal data when it is **strictly necessary** for the detection and correction of biases, provided that:
- a) **Synthetic or anonymized** data do not allow the detection/correction to be carried out
- b) They are subject to **technical limitations** on reuse and **security and pseudonymization** measures
- c) They are subject to **adequate safeguards**, strict controls, and access documentation
- d) They are **not transmitted** to third parties
- e) They are **deleted** once the bias has been corrected or at the end of the retention period
- f) The **activity records** include the reasons why the processing was strictly necessary

### 3.4 Systems without training (Art. 10.6)

For high-risk systems that do not use model training, paragraphs 2 to 5 shall apply only to **test datasets**.

## 4. Data management model

The proposed model is organized into five sequential phases with continuous monitoring and improvement:

### 4.1 Information requirements

- Establish **what information** the AI system needs to achieve its objective.
- First phase of the life cycle: identify the data needed to address the need or solve the problem.

### 4.2 Data collection

- Obtain data from **multiple sources** to avoid dependencies and biases.
- Select the most reliable source (source of truth) with a **Data Owner** and quality control model at the source.
- Collection methods: automated (web scraping, APIs, crawling), transactions between systems, sensors/IoT, new contexts, subscription, public/private crowdsourcing.
- Consider **personal data protection** regulations at all times.

### 4.3 Data preparation

Processing and conditioning of collected data. Includes the following operations:

#### 4.3.1 Measurement and improvement of data quality

Five-phase methodology:

| Phase | Description |
|---|---|
| **A. Determine quality dimensions** | Select the relevant dimensions for each data element (completeness, representativeness, consistency, relevance, diversity, credibility, etc.) |
| **B. Define quality controls** | Establish metrics for each dimension (e.g., ratio of reported data, consistency rules). See Annex B.2 |
| **C. Implement the controls** | Translate the controls into the technical language of each platform (Excel/VBA, SQL, Python, etc.) |
| **D. Report the results** | Reports with context, reported data, quality requirements, execution times, responsible parties, storage |
| **E. Develop improvement measures** | Inventory incidents, prioritize by criticality, reach consensus with stakeholders, define a remediation plan |

- Quality controls must be defined at **each point of the data life cycle**.
- Establish **minimum quality requirements** for each control (e.g., 100% completeness if the data element is essential).

#### 4.3.2 Data transformation

- Conversion to a **homogenized format** (homogenization, normalization, scaling).
- Necessary when data are collected from various sources with different formats or units.

#### 4.3.3 Data aggregation

- Group data to draw conclusions about the information they represent.
- Aggregated data must have the **same quality control scheme**.

#### 4.3.4 Data sampling

- Extract representative subsets from the dataset.
- Two main techniques: **random sampling** and **stratified sampling** (to ensure adequate representation of subgroups).

#### 4.3.5 Feature creation and selection

- **Creation:** Increase dimensionality by combining existing features or estimating new ones.
- **Selection:** Reduce dimensionality by selecting the most relevant features. Techniques: PCA (Principal Component Analysis).

#### 4.3.6 Data enrichment

- Expand available data by incorporating data from **new sources**.
- Process subsequent to the initial collection, to cover additional identified needs.

#### 4.3.7 Data labeling

- Assign identifying labels to collected data (necessary in supervised learning).
- Four approaches: **internal** (organization experts), **external** (contracting), **crowdsourcing** (web platforms), **automatic** (predefined rules with human oversight by sampling).
- Consider the integration of **Human-in-the-loop** (HITL) in the process.
- Crowdsourcing poses a possible **attack vector** (training data poisoning).

#### 4.3.8 Analysis of biases in data

Three-phase process:

1. **Analysis of bias sources:** Identify the main sources (see Annex C.1: selection bias, sampling bias, coverage bias, participation bias, labeling bias, incompleteness bias, preparation bias, Simpson's paradox, confounding variables, non-normality, aggregation bias, outliers).
2. **Bias evaluation:** Apply evaluation techniques (see Annex C.2: classification metrics, equalized odds, equality of opportunity, statistical parity, predictive equality).
3. **Bias treatment:** Implement mitigation measures (see Annex C.3: pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing methods).

- Bias **is not always negative**; what matters is having the ability to **identify it and evaluate its impact**.
- Bias analysis is an **essential** process, not optional.

### 4.4 Data disposition

- Provide the prepared data for use in the AI system.
- Elements to consider: specify where and how they are stored, authentication and access, version control, update records, report the presence of personal data, security/privacy measures, possible misuse, metadata and statistical properties.

### 4.5 Data deletion

Last phase of the life cycle. Two options:

- **Data transfer:** Identify the recipient with adequate safeguards, document the agreement, verify legal compliance, generate a report.
- **Data deletion:** Verify that no one requires access, legal compliance, deletion in all locations, evaluate the possibility of restoration from the model, review cultural/historical importance, generate a report.
- **Partial deletion** (e.g., GDPR requests): Evaluate the impact on the remaining dataset, document and inform stakeholders, delete in all locations, generate a report.

## 5. Processing of special categories of personal data

### 5.1 Special categories (Art. 10.5)

Special categories of personal data include:
- Racial or ethnic origin
- Political opinions
- Religious or philosophical beliefs
- Trade union membership
- Genetic data
- Biometric data
- Health data, sex life, or sexual orientation

**Legal basis:** Art. 10.5 of the AI Regulation is the **specific European provision** that allows the processing of these categories based on a **substantial public interest** (detection and correction of biases to mitigate discrimination).

### 5.2 Security and privacy measures

- **Anonymization:** Initial premise — whenever possible, anonymize. If anonymization significantly affects bias detection, document and justify.
- **Pseudonymization:** When anonymization would significantly affect bias detection. Replace identifiers with pseudonyms, protect the link with additional information.
- **Other safeguards:** Procedures to identify/eliminate biases, access rights management, encryption in transmission to third parties, verify the absence of historical biases in training data, human oversight mechanisms.

### 5.3 Sandbox: Art. 10.5 not applicable until August 2026

Until Art. 10 becomes fully applicable (August 2, 2026), the processing of special categories **cannot be based on this article**. Providers must seek other avenues of legitimation under the applicable data protection legislation in force.

## 6. Data quality dimensions (Annex B.1)

The guide details 18 quality dimensions:

| # | Dimension | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accessibility | Degree of access to data in a specific context |
| 2 | Auditability | Degree of review or availability for auditing |
| 3 | Identifiability | Ability to identify PII in the dataset |
| 4 | Portability | Ability to move data between systems while preserving quality |
| 5 | Comprehensibility | Ease with which data can be interpreted |
| 6 | Temporal coherence | Alignment of data with current reality |
| 7 | Effectiveness | Degree to which the dataset meets requirements for its use in AI |
| 8 | Efficiency | Degree to which data can be processed with appropriate resources |
| 9 | Accuracy | Degree to which each data element has the correct value (syntactic and semantic) |
| 10 | Completeness | Degree to which data have values for all features |
| 11 | Regulatory compliance | Degree of compliance with applicable regulations |
| 12 | Credibility | Degree to which data are considered true and believable |
| 13 | Balance | Distribution of samples across all aspects of the dataset |
| 14 | Consistency | Degree to which data do not present contradictions or inconsistencies |
| 15 | Diversity | Difference between samples in terms of target data |
| 16 | Relevance | Degree of adequacy of the dataset for a given context |
| 17 | Representativeness | Degree to which a sample reflects the target population |
| 18 | Similarity | Degree of similarity between samples in terms of features |

Each dimension has associated **quantitative quality controls** (Annex B.2) expressed as ratios (X = A/B).

## 7. Sources of bias (Annex C.1)

### Biases inherent to data

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Selection/sampling bias | Mismatch between selected data and their actual distribution |
| Coverage bias | Mismatch between the training dataset population and the real population |
| Participation bias | Unequal participation rates between groups |
| Labeling bias | Inadequate discretization or cognitive biases of the labeler |
| Incompleteness bias | Completeness imbalance between populations |
| Preparation bias | Imputation/cleaning decisions that introduce bias |
| Simpson's paradox | Trend that reverses when combining datasets |
| Confounding variables | Variables that influence both dependent and independent variables |
| Non-normality | Data subject to non-normal distributions |
| Aggregation bias | Aggregating data with different statistical distributions |
| Outliers and noise | Extreme values or stochastic components |

### Human cognitive biases

Automation bias, group attribution bias, implicit bias, in-group/out-group bias, out-group homogeneity bias, confirmation bias, social bias.

## 8. Bias evaluation techniques (Annex C.2)

| Technique | Description |
|---|---|
| Classification systems | Divide data by groups, calculate bias metrics per group, compare |
| Reinforcement learning systems | Apply utility metrics to all groups |
| Confusion matrix | TP, FP, FN, TN and derived metrics (TPR, FPR, precision, recall, F1) |
| Equalized odds | TPR and FPR equal across all demographic categories |
| Equality of opportunity | TPR equal across all categories |
| Statistical parity | Equal prediction rates between categories |
| Predictive equality | FPR equal across all categories |

## 9. Bias treatment measures (Annex C.3)

Organized by life cycle stage:

| Stage | Measures |
|---|---|
| **1. Requirements analysis** | External requirements (human rights, privacy, competition), internal requirements, inclusion of transdisciplinary experts, identification of stakeholders, selection and documentation of sources, external change, acceptance criteria |
| **2. Design and development** | Data representation and labeling, transparency tools (model cards), training data (feature removal, reweighting, stratified sampling, feature selection), testing (grouping, visualization, separate teams), tuning (data-based/model-based/post-hoc methods, mitigation algorithms), adversarial methods |
| **3. Verification and validation** | Static data analysis, label control samples, internal validity tests, external validity tests, user tests, exploratory tests |
| **4. Deployment** | Training of deployers, instructions for end users, transparency tools on known biases |
| **4.1 Continuous validation** | Continuous performance monitoring, bias and fairness metrics, retraining or redesign if indicators are detected |

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Data governance is a design requirement, not an afterthought:** Art. 10 requires that training, validation, and test datasets be subject to adequate data governance and management practices for the system's intended purpose. This must be planned from the first phase of the life cycle.

2. **Five-phase data management model:** Information requirements, collection, preparation, disposition, and deletion. Each phase has specific processes and all are subject to continuous monitoring and improvement.

3. **Measurement and improvement of data quality is essential:** It is not an optional process. It requires a systematic methodology: determine quality dimensions, define quantitative controls, implement them, report results, and develop improvement measures. The guide details 18 quality dimensions with specific controls.

4. **18 data quality dimensions:** From accessibility and completeness to representativeness and diversity, each dimension must be evaluated individually for each data element. Dimensions such as balance and representativeness are critical for avoiding biases.

5. **Bias analysis is mandatory and systematic:** A three-phase process — analyze bias sources, evaluate bias with formal metrics (equalized odds, statistical parity, etc.), and apply treatment measures. Bias is not always negative; what is critical is identifying it and evaluating its impact.

6. **Sources of bias at each stage of the life cycle:** Biases can originate in data selection, sampling, coverage, labeling, preparation, and even in human cognitive biases (automation, confirmation, group attribution). Each source requires specific mitigation measures.

7. **Special categories of personal data: exceptional and conditional use:** Processing of sensitive data (race, health, sexual orientation, etc.) is only permitted when strictly necessary to detect and correct biases, and when synthetic or anonymized data are insufficient. It requires pseudonymization, access controls, prohibition of transfer to third parties, and subsequent deletion.

8. **Anonymization as the initial premise, pseudonymization as the alternative:** Whenever possible, anonymize special categories. If anonymization significantly affects bias detection, document the justification and proceed with pseudonymization with all safeguards.

9. **Collect data from multiple sources to avoid dependencies:** Using a single source creates the risk that the system's accuracy depends on that source. Select the most reliable source as the source of truth, with a Data Owner and quality control at the source.

10. **Data labeling: consider HITL and security risks:** Automatic labeling must be accompanied by human validation and oversight. Crowdsourcing poses a possible attack vector (data poisoning). In supervised learning, labeling quality is determinant for model accuracy.

11. **Data deletion with a rigorous process:** Both full and partial deletion (GDPR requests) require evaluating the impact on the remaining dataset, documenting the process, informing stakeholders, and ensuring deletion in all locations.

12. **Complete technical documentation of data governance:** At a minimum, data technical sheets (methodologies, datasets, origin, labeling, cleaning) and supervision and control information (capabilities, limitations, accuracy, risks, human oversight measures). Document each measure implemented with its responsible party.

**Illustrative use cases:** Granting financial aid to families without resources, Intelligent insulin pump

## 1. Concept of transparency in AI

Transparency in AI is defined as the quality of an AI system of being entirely **interpretable and comprehensible** by all persons who create it and interact with it **throughout its entire life cycle** — from conceptualization and design, during its implementation, and when it is in operation.

- It allows understanding the reasoning behind the system's outputs and controlling it appropriately.
- To achieve comprehension and transparency, it is **necessary to resort to Explainability techniques**.
- The concept is linked to **Explainability** and **Interpretability**, used in an integrated manner.
- Transparency is **the foundation upon which trust in AI is built**.

### Two dimensions of transparency

1. **For those responsible:** That those responsible for the creation, operation, and exploitation of the system can **understand how it works**.
2. **For users/affected persons:** That end users or deployers can **interpret the results**, knowing on what criteria a prediction or decision was made, and under what circumstances it could have been different.

## 4. Applicable measures to achieve transparency

The guide defines **12 proprietary transparency measures** (MT1-MT12) plus 3 measures linked to other articles of the Regulation (MT13-MT15). They are categorized into two types:

- **Design measures:** Require human decision and planning (contact, functional domain, functional objective, prudence, causality, information channel).
- **Automatable measures:** Can be supported by existing technologies (data transparency, global-to-specific detail, adapted language, integrated metrics, counterfactuality).

### 4.1 Provide contact with the provider (MT1)

- Establish a **clear point of contact** between the provider and the deployer.
- **Proactive** functions: monitor regulatory compliance, update policies, learning degradation due to new scenarios.
- **Reactive** functions: address requests and incidents from the deployer.
- Contact channel: IT demand management system, with logging, workflows, monitoring, and incident history.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 3a (Contact), Paragraph 3c (Changes).

### 4.2 Address the functional domain (MT2)

- Identify the **domain-specific transparency needs** (Insurance, Finance, Healthcare, Retail, Transport, Industry, Public Administration).
- The needs differ depending on the domain and specific use case, even within the same type of high-risk system.
- Consider the specific transparency legislation applicable (particularly for Public Administration).

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.i (Intended purpose), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.3 Ensure the functional objective of the system (MT3)

- Identify **foreseeable circumstances of unintended uses** and define a risk plan for them.
- Proactively monitor that such uses do not occur during operation.
- Transparently inform the patient/user about what the product is, who prescribes it, and how it is calculated.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.iii (Risks), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.4 Transparency about the data used (MT4)

- **Enumerate the data sources** used by the system (both for learning and for use).
- Perform an **Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)** of said sources: meta-information, critical values, outliers, relationships between data.
- Applicable both to the deployer (data owner) and the provider (analysis tools).

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.v (Impact on persons), Paragraph 3b.vi (Input data), Paragraph 3b.vii (Output information), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.5 Detail from the most global to the most specific (MT5)

- Internal information must be **concise and as complete as possible**.
- Understand operation from the most **general to the most specific**, enabling technical measures that allow addressing three levels of detail:
  1. **Global reasoning mechanism** of the system.
  2. **Predictions/decisions on subsets** of information with similar characteristics (homogeneity).
  3. **Individual predictions/decisions** (each specific case).

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.6 Adapt the language (MT6)

The system must provide information adapted to the **level of communication** of each profile:

| Profile | Information Need |
|---|---|
| **Deployers** | Natural language, homogeneous predictions, business focus, dashboards |
| **Provider technicians** | Technical language, model reasoning, regulatory compliance |
| **Affected persons** | Natural language without technical jargon, reasons for decisions, alternative conditions |

- The provider supplies technical mechanisms and interfaces; the deployer adapts them to their business solution.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.7 Manage complexity (MT7)

- **Tradeoff between accuracy and transparency:** Increasing model complexity can improve accuracy but reduces transparency.
- It is advisable to choose the **simplest model** that meets the business objectives.
- **White-box models** (regressions, decision trees): directly transparent.
- **Black-box models** (neural networks, random forests): their transparency is limited; this may imply **having to discard them** in high-risk applications.
- For black-box models: partial solution through algorithms such as *profweight* that generate an equivalent interpretable system.
- Simplicity also reduces **energy consumption** (environmental impact).

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.ii (Level of accuracy), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.8 Use metrics integrated into the system's life cycle (MT8)

- Define specific metrics on **data, model, result quality, and performance** (in accordance with ISO/IEC 23053).
- Validate metrics in final testing **before putting the system into operation**.
- **Monitor during operation**, specifying minimum acceptable values to take action (retrain, stop execution).
- Detect **data drift** (degradation due to new data) and **model drift** (changes in the model).
- Associated with a **continuous integration system (MLOps)** to analyze each version/release.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.ii (Level of accuracy), Paragraph 3c (Changes), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight), Paragraph 3e (HW/SW resources).

### 4.9 Apply prudence (MT9)

The information provided must be **relevant but prudent**. It should not be made public if:
- It leads to **greater confusion** for users.
- It can be exploited for **poisoning attacks** against the system.
- It reveals **private, confidential, or GDPR-protected information**.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.10 Use causality, minimize correlations (MT10)

- **Correlation does not imply causation**: prioritize variables with a direct causal relationship.
- Correlations can complicate transparency and the level of accuracy.
- Identify valid and **explicitly undesired** correlations (sources of bias).
- The deployer identifies correlations from the business perspective; the provider documents how they have been implemented and their impact on accuracy.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.ii (Level of accuracy), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight).

### 4.11 Use counterfactuality (MT11)

- It is not enough to detail the reason for a decision: also **why it was that one and not another** (counterfactuality).
- Explain under what circumstances the action could have been different if some information were modified.
- Especially relevant for **persons subject to the system's decisions**, who have the right to know the criteria.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 1 (Design and development), Paragraph 3b.vii (Output information), Paragraph 3d (Human oversight), Paragraph 3b.v (Impact on persons).

### 4.12 Enable a channel with the system's usage information (MT12)

- In addition to the system itself, provide an **external medium** (web/wiki/doc page) oriented toward transparency.
- Suggested content: provider contact, system's intended purpose, recommended and non-recommended use cases, performance/accuracy, potential biases, possible attacks, data used, log files, life cycle, version changelog.

**Applies to:** Paragraph 2 (Instructions for use).

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Transparency is a design requirement, not post-hoc documentation:** High-risk AI systems must be designed and developed from the outset with a sufficient level of transparency so that deployers can interpret and correctly use the results (Art. 13.1). Transparency measures must be integrated into the methodological and software development tools.

2. **Two dimensions of transparency that must be covered simultaneously:** Understanding how it works (for those responsible for the system) and interpreting the results (for users and affected persons). Both require specific and differentiated technical measures.

3. **Seven specific pieces of information mandatory in instructions for use:** Provider contact (a), characteristics/capabilities/limitations with seven sub-elements (b), default changes (c), human oversight measures (d), HW/SW resources and maintenance (e), and log file mechanisms (f).

4. **Adapt the language to the interlocutor's profile:** The system must provide comprehensible information for three distinct profiles: deployers (natural business language), provider technicians (technical language), and affected persons (natural language without technical jargon with explanations of reasons and conditions).

5. **Critical tradeoff between complexity and transparency:** Increasing model complexity can improve accuracy but reduces transparency. For high-risk systems, the simplest model that meets the objectives is recommended. Black-box models (neural networks) may have to be discarded if sufficient transparency cannot be achieved.

6. **Counterfactuality as a transparency tool:** It is not enough to explain why a decision was made; the system must be able to explain under what circumstances the decision would have been different. This is especially relevant for persons subject to automated decisions.

7. **Metrics integrated into the life cycle with minimum thresholds:** Define metrics on data, model, and performance, validate them before production, and continuously monitor them. Detect data drift and model drift to take action (retrain or stop the system). Associate with an MLOps continuous integration system.

8. **Prudence in the information disclosed:** Transparency must be relevant but must not generate confusion, facilitate poisoning attacks against the system, or reveal private, confidential, or GDPR-protected information. The deployer identifies sensitive information; the provider implements the filters.

9. **Causality over correlation:** Prioritize direct causal relationships over correlations, which can complicate transparency and be sources of bias. Identify and document both valid and explicitly undesired correlations.

10. **Mandatory external information channel for the system:** In addition to the transparency integrated into the system, enable an external medium (web/wiki/doc page) that compiles information about the operation: contact, intended purpose, use cases, performance, biases, data, logs, life cycle, and changelog.

11. **Human oversight requires all transparency measures:** Paragraph 3d (human oversight) is linked to virtually all transparency measures (10 of 12), since for the system to be overseen by persons, it is fundamental that it be transparent.

12. **32 minimum transparency documents:** The guide identifies specific documentation organized by measure, covering both the provider's obligations (technical manuals, tools) and the deployer's obligations (requirements, analysis evidence, reports) for each sub-paragraph of Art. 13.
# Guide 9. Accuracy

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Technical standard under development:** prEN 18229-2 "AI Trustworthiness Framework — Part 2: Accuracy and Robustness"
**Core article:** Art. 15 AIA (paragraphs 1, 2, and 3 — those related to accuracy)
**Illustrative use cases:** False report detection, Employee promotion system

---

## 1. Concept of accuracy in AI

Accuracy is a quantitative measure of the relationship between the **intended purpose** of the AI system and its **actual performance**, from design through deployment.

- Accuracy is among the fundamental requirements for **risk mitigation** associated with the AI system (Recital 66).
- The level of accuracy must be **up to date with advances and state of the art** in the field and maintained throughout the entire lifecycle (Recital 74).
- Accuracy is closely related to: cybersecurity, robustness, transparency, data governance, and human oversight.
- **Terminological note:** In this guide, *accuracy* is used as the primary term. When *accuracy* forms part of a compound technical term in English (e.g., *balanced accuracy*), the English term is retained to avoid confusion.

---

| Regulation Article | Requirement | Guide Section |
|---|---|---|
| 15.1 | Adequate level of accuracy | Section 4.1 and Section 4.3 |
| 15.1 | Consistent accuracy throughout the lifecycle | Section 4.1 |
| 15.2 | Technical aspects of accuracy | Section 5 |
| 15.3 | Instructions and relevant accuracy levels | Section 5 |

---

## 3. Requirements of Art. 15 (paragraphs related to accuracy)

### 3.1 Art. 15.1 — Adequate level and consistency

High-risk AI systems shall be designed and developed in such a way as to achieve an **adequate level of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity** and to perform consistently throughout their lifecycle.

### 3.2 Art. 15.2 — Technical aspects of measurement

The Commission, in cooperation with relevant stakeholders and metrology authorities, shall encourage the development of **benchmarks and measurement methodologies** for accuracy and robustness.

### 3.3 Art. 15.3 — Instructions for use

The instructions for use shall indicate the **accuracy levels** of the system and the **relevant parameters** for measuring it.

---

## 4. Accuracy and lifecycle

Establishing and measuring accuracy is a process that spans the **entire lifecycle**. The process follows an iterative flow: Assessment → Data preprocessing / Overfitting avoidance / Use of appropriate models / Uncertainty calibration → Metric selection → Metric application → Continuous monitoring and review.

### 4.1 Data preprocessing

- Test data must be **representative** of the intended purpose.
- Training data must be **free from sampling bias**.
- Maintain **similar distributions** between training, validation, and evaluation data when splitting unbalanced datasets.
- Use the **same preprocessing** to compare models and the **same evaluation (test) set**.
- Validation and evaluation sets **shall never contain samples from the training set**.
- Document pretraining tasks accurately and reproducibly.

### 4.2 Overfitting

- Report **all hyperparameters** in training/testing and validation processes.
- **No information from the test set** should be used to tune hyperparameters (avoid optimistic overestimation).
- Use **nested cross-validation** to separate method selection (outer loop) from hyperparameter tuning (inner loop).
- Use **automated hyperparameter search tools** (Hyperband, Hyperopt, Optuna).

### 4.3 Use of appropriate models

- Compare with **state-of-the-art baseline models** to contextualize accuracy.
- Conduct **ablation studies** to justify the composition and complexity of the model.
- Trivial baselines (e.g., always predicting the majority class) are useful for calibration but **should not be the only point of comparison**.

### 4.4 Uncertainty and accuracy

- Accuracy has an associated **level of certainty or confidence** that does not always accompany model outputs.
- The system may provide a **confidence indicator** (e.g., percentage) of the result.
- Uncertainty must be **documented** to ensure accuracy and facilitate oversight and transparency.

---

## 5. Evaluating accuracy

### 5.1 Provider responsibilities

- Accuracy metrics are **controls based on the intended purpose** and oriented toward the mitigation of risks to rights and freedoms.
- The provider selects metrics from the **conception and design** phase, considering:
  - Relationship with the **intended purpose**.
  - Ability to **mitigate risks** identified in the risk analysis.
- Define a **battery of tests** that are meaningful and representative of the domain: unit/stress tests, integration tests, accuracy monitoring in production, monitoring dashboards.
- Provide detailed documentation on installation, preprocessing, postprocessing, configurable parameter ranges, and expected latency measurements.
- Documentation and formatting in the open standard **ONNX** is recommended for reproducibility.

### 5.2 Selection of accuracy metrics

Two fundamental aspects for selection:
1. The **intended purpose** of the system.
2. The **risks identified** in the risk management system.

- Maintain a **centralized repository** to manage metrics associated with the model at any point in the lifecycle.
- The deployer must be able to **access and monitor** the metrics and protected variables.

### 5.3 Selection of the objective function

- The objective functions to optimize (minimize or maximize) are determined by the **specific problems** that the system must solve.
- The objective function must allow the accuracy metric to **achieve the intended purpose**.
- Document motivations, approaches, and results of the selection.

### 5.4 Dimensions complementary to accuracy

- **Monitor** accuracy metrics throughout the system's lifecycle to detect deterioration.
- **Bias, fairness, and explainability** metrics are complementary and particularly relevant.
- Fairness and explainability tools are preferably **open source** based on the principle of transparency.
- The deployer must have access to:
  - An interface for accuracy and performance metrics to report deficiencies.
  - Inspection of input and output data.

### 5.5 Deployer

- Must **know and understand** the accuracy level of the system.
- Train staff to interpret visualizations of accuracy, explainability, and uncertainty.
- Understand the concepts of **algorithmic discrimination, biases, and fairness** to detect degradations.

---

## 6. Ensuring accuracy

### 6.1 Technical measures

- Documentation must include **configurable parameter ranges**, input/output data, and expected **latency** measurements.
- The system's output must be accompanied by an **uncertainty measure**.
- Store all accuracy values in a **historical record** (in accordance with the Records Guide) for traceability.
- Provide a **graphical interface** to the deployer for observing metrics over time.
- Document the reasoning for selecting each metric (in accordance with the Technical Documentation Guide).
- Use of **ensemble learning models** to establish confidence values based on the responses of different models.

### 6.2 Statistical significance assessments

Specific metrics must be accompanied by statistical assessments that consider data distribution and dependencies:

- **Wilcoxon signed-rank test** — non-parametric, free of assumptions about distribution.
- **Paired Student's T-Test**, analysis of variances, Kruskal-Wallis, Chi-Squared, Fisher's exact, McNemar (including Bonferroni correction and false discovery rate).
- **ANOVA** to determine whether differences in accuracy between 3 or more models are statistically significant.

### 6.3 Database and model benchmarks

- Conduct **benchmarks** (performance tests) of data and models taking into account the intended purpose.
- Use and document the benchmarks most recognized by the scientific community.
- Benchmarks must be **updated** as models and databases evolve.

Examples of benchmarks by model type:

| Model type | Databases | Baseline models |
|---|---|---|
| Computer vision | ImageNet, MNIST, CIFAR-10, LFW | AlexNet, VGG-19, ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B7, LeNet, RDML, MCDNN, FaceNet, DeepID3 |
| Language models | — | LSTM, BERT, GPT1/2/3+ |
| Image generative models | — | Metrics: SSIM, MMD, IS, MS, FID, LPIPS; IoU for segmentation |
| Fairness | COMPAS recid, COMPAS viol. recid, Diabetes, OULAD, Credit card clients | — |

## 7. Technical documentation

Accuracy documentation must cover the following points of Annex IV of the Regulation:

- **Point 2(g):** Validation and testing procedures, parameters for measuring accuracy, robustness, and compliance, potentially discriminatory effects, log files, dated and signed reports.
- **Point 3:** Accuracy levels achieved and expected, differentiated by groups or contexts, methodology, known limitations, margins of error, and justification of adequacy.
- **Point 4:** Justification of the suitability of the metrics for the intended use of the system.
- **Point 9:** Post-market continuous monitoring system, including accuracy monitoring and corrective actions.

### 7.1 Model Card

A descriptive document of the model that must include at minimum:

- Model size (number of parameters).
- Publication date.
- Model type (from scratch or base model).
- Intended purpose.
- Identity terms (reference to vulnerable groups: sexual orientation, gender, race).
- Training, validation, and evaluation details (performance, efficiency, limitations).
- Ethical implications, risks, and recommendations.

Accuracy-specific sections in the card:

- **Selected metrics** and why they were chosen.
- **Decision thresholds** and their justification (interactive slider bar in digital format).
- **Uncertainty and variability:** standard deviation, variance, confidence intervals, KL divergence.
- **Calculation methods:** mean of runs, cross-validation, iterations.
- **Interpretability:** SHAP, LIME, feature importance.
- **Adaptability:** fine-tuning capability or periodic updating.
- **Responsible use:** warnings about limitations and inappropriate contexts.

### 7.2 Datasheet for Datasets

- Holistic view of accuracy metrics and the provenance of training data.
- Document potential **intersectional disparities** in accuracy.
- Document bias tests and **disparate impact** for different groups.

### 8.2 Classification models

- **Image models:** IoU (segmentation), Frechet Inception Distance (FID), Inception Score (IS), sharpness.

- **Generative models (GANs):** min-max loss, non-saturating GAN loss, Wasserstein GAN loss, Sharpness Loss, Cook's Distance, conditional GAN functions (CGAN); for VAE: Log Hyperbolic Cosine Loss (LogCosh).

## 10. Accuracy, biases, and fairness (Annex 7.3)

### 10.1 Biases and accuracy

The presence of biases can **distort accuracy metrics** even without an incorrect bias analysis. Verify and mitigate biases throughout the entire lifecycle, especially in:

- Data and their operation and output.
- Establishment of accuracy metrics.
- Model development and validation.

Types of biases to monitor:
- Representation bias, historical bias, population bias, sampling bias, omitted variable bias.
- Masking bias, intersectional bias, algorithmic bias.
- Selection bias, collider bias, information bias, recall bias, ascertainment bias, attrition bias, immortal time bias, confounding bias, Hawthorne effect.

### 10.2 Fairness to mitigate biases

When a bias is detected, apply **fairness techniques** based on differences, ratios, and statistical fairness assessments.

**Group fairness notions:**
- Class independence (statistical parity).
- Class separation: Equalized Odds, equal opportunity, predictive equality, total fairness.
- Class sufficiency: conditional use accuracy equality, predictive parity, Well calibration.
- Total fairness, treatment equality, overall accuracy equality.

**Fairness techniques:**
- Reweighting to mitigate bias in training data.
- Balanced accuracy, average odds difference to avoid disparate impact.
- Area under absolute ROC curves for protected and unprotected groups (ABROCA).
- Fairness ontology to determine applicable metrics.
- Plotly sunburst plots for visualization of biases stratified by protected attributes.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Accuracy is a full lifecycle requirement, not a one-time measurement:** Art. 15.1 requires that accuracy be achieved and maintained consistently throughout the entire lifecycle of the system. The provider must establish continuous monitoring mechanisms to detect and correct degradations.

2. **Select metrics driven by the intended purpose and risks:** Accuracy metrics must be directly related to what the system aims to do and to the risks identified in the risk management system. An inadequate metric can give false assurance about actual performance.

3. **The objective function must align with the accuracy metric:** The provider selects an objective function that allows the model to achieve the required accuracy for the intended purpose. Document the motivation and results of this selection.

4. **Prevent overfitting with methodological rigor:** Report all hyperparameters, do not use information from the test set to tune them, maintain completely disjoint training/validation/test sets, and use nested cross-validation or automated hyperparameter search tools.

5. **Accompany accuracy with uncertainty measures:** Each system output should include a confidence indicator. Uncertainty must be documented and can be estimated using techniques such as ensemble learning to provide confidence values based on multiple models.

6. **Contextualize with baselines and benchmarks:** Compare the system's accuracy with baseline reference models and with benchmarks recognized by the scientific community, updating them as the state of the art evolves. Conduct ablation studies to justify model complexity.

7. **Validate with statistical significance:** Accuracy metrics must be accompanied by statistical assessments (Wilcoxon, T-Test, ANOVA, etc.) that consider data distribution and dependencies, to confirm that performance differences are significant and not due to chance.

8. **Monitor biases as an inseparable dimension of accuracy:** Biases can distort accuracy metrics. Verify and mitigate representation, historical, sampling, algorithmic, and intersectional biases throughout the entire lifecycle, especially after deployment to production.

9. **Document accuracy through Model Card and Datasheet for Datasets:** The model card must include selected metrics, decision thresholds, uncertainty, calculation methods, interpretability, and usage warnings. The datasheet for datasets complements this with data provenance and intersectional disparities.

10. **Differentiated responsibilities for provider/deployer:** The provider selects metrics, implements technical measures, provides monitoring interfaces and instructions. The deployer must understand accuracy, train their staff, monitor the system, and notify the provider of degradations.

11. **Fairness as a mandatory complement to accuracy:** Fairness notions (statistical parity, equalized odds, predictive parity, etc.) must be implemented through specific metrics and aligned with the risk scales of the risk management system. Document trade-offs between fairness principles when not all can be satisfied simultaneously.

12. **Accuracy is not complete without transparency:** Model accuracy will not be complete if transparency is not provided about how metrics are produced and computed. The deployer must have access to interfaces that display metrics, explainability, and uncertainty at any point in the lifecycle.
# Guide 10. Robustness

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Technical standard under development:** prEN 18229-2 "AI Trustworthiness Framework — Part 2: Accuracy and Robustness"
**Core article:** Art. 15 AIA (paragraphs 1 and 4 for robustness)
**Illustrative use cases:** False report detection, Employee promotion system

---

## 1. Concept of robustness in AI

Robustness is the ability of a high-risk AI system to **minimize and prevent harmful and undesirable behaviors**, to detect when its operation falls outside the intended domain, and to avoid erroneous decisions or incorrect output information. It is expressed through interrelated properties:

- **Reliability:** Consistency between initially estimated values and subsequent ones. Robustness is a sub-characteristic of reliability (ISO/IEC 25059:2023, SQuaRE method).
- **Stability:** The degree to which the system's output changes in a controlled manner when inputs vary within a specific domain. Relevant in classification and identification tasks.
- **Sensitivity:** The degree to which the output varies when inputs undergo changes. Relevant in systems that require direct accuracy with respect to a ground truth (e.g., insulin pump).
- **Relevance:** The magnitude of the impact that different inputs have on outputs. Useful in applications where the AI performs tasks that a human could perform.
- **Reachability:** Multi-step accuracy of an AI system in interaction with its environment. Applies to agent-based systems (perception-action-environment cycle).

### Local vs. global robustness

- **Local robustness:** Verifies properties within the input and output domains of the system in relation to its intended purpose. Easier to specify. Guarantees are local to the tested sample.
- **Global robustness:** Defines deterministic guarantees over all possible inputs, specifying valid ranges for input features. More difficult to achieve.
- Both are equally important and must be verified for high-risk systems.

### Relationship with other requirements

Robustness is closely related to:
- **Accuracy** (Art. 15): Robustness seeks to preserve the accuracy obtained during training, testing, and validation throughout the lifecycle.
- **Cybersecurity** (Art. 15): Cybersecurity contributes to robustness by protecting against adversarial attacks; robustness mechanisms must ensure that cybersecurity measures are maintained over time.
- **Human oversight** and **Transparency**: Robustness must be understood in relation to the oversight and transparency of the system.

|---|---|---|

| 15.4 paragraph 1 | Resistance to errors, faults, and inconsistencies | Section 4.2 |

## 3. Requirements of Art. 15 (robustness)

### 3.1 Adequate and consistent level (Art. 15.1)

High-risk AI systems shall be designed and developed in such a way as to achieve an **adequate level of robustness** and perform consistently throughout their lifecycle. They must not present operational problems (compatibility) or quality issues. They shall achieve and maintain **reasonable minimum levels of robustness** pre-established in the requirements.

### 3.2 Resistance to errors, faults, and inconsistencies (Art. 15.4 paragraph 1)

High-risk AI systems shall be as **resistant as possible to errors, faults, or inconsistencies** that may arise in the systems themselves or in the environment, in particular due to their interaction with natural persons or other systems. Technical and organizational measures shall be adopted.

### 3.3 Technical redundancy (Art. 15.4 paragraph 2)

Robustness may be achieved through **technical redundancy** solutions, such as **backup copies** or **fail-safe plans**.

### 3.4 Systems that continue learning (Art. 15.4 paragraph 3)

Systems that continue learning after being placed on the market shall be developed in such a way as to eliminate or reduce the risk that biased outputs influence the input information of future operations (**feedback loops**), and to ensure that such loops are addressed with appropriate **risk mitigation measures**.

---

## 4. Evaluation of robustness

### 4.1 Metrics and lifecycle

Robustness must be verified throughout the entire lifecycle:

| Phase | Responsible party | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| **Design and development** | Provider | Identify characteristics, verify separability, design experiments, use ensemble learning for adversarial resistance |
| **Verification and validation (V&V)** | Provider | Verify covered parts of the domain, measure impact of perturbations, validate with real data |
| **Deployment to production** | Provider + Deployer | Verify impact of numerical precision, ensure continuity between environments (containers) |

**Methodology for metric selection** (6 steps):
1. Establish robustness requirements or objectives and associated metrics
2. Plan experiments that demonstrate robustness
3. Conduct the experiments recording results
4. Analyze results against the established metrics
5. Interpret results progressively (evolution over time)
6. Determine robustness; if insufficient, iterate

### 4.2 Verification and Validation

- **Verification:** Confirmation through objective evidence that specified requirements have been met (ISO/IEC 25000:2014). Does not require execution with real data.
- **Validation:** Confirmation that the requirements for the intended purpose have been met (ISO/IEC 25000:2014). Includes testing with real datasets.
- Data processed by different architectures may be analyzed by at least one **formal method** (ISO/IEC 24029-2:2023).

### 4.3 Efficiency in AI systems

When computational or memory constraints exist, the following are recommended:
1. **Edge ML** (edge machine learning) for devices with limited resources
2. **Distillation** when there are memory limitations
3. **Distilled privileged learning** when not all data modalities are available at inference
4. **Continual learning** when the model cannot grow in architecture
5. **Network compression and pruning techniques**

### 4.4 Performance in AI systems

Performance metrics associated with robustness:
- **Performance per power:** watts, joules per watt, or nanojoules per pixel
- **Computational complexity:** classification latency, classification efficiency, energy consumption
- **Processing performance:** FLOPS, FLOPY, latency, efficiency (images/second)

Considerations:
- Hardware and architectures must be transparent and fixed in the documentation
- Development, verification/validation, and production environments must be identical or with specifically equivalent robustness conditions
- Define minimum hardware requirements in the system instructions

### 4.5 Robustness monitoring

- Observe statistics, data distribution, and changes in business usage
- Provide feedback mechanisms to distinguish deployment errors vs. system errors vs. context errors
- Record error types in a problem resolution process
- Audit models based on ethical principles (evaluation of Stability, Sensitivity, Relevance, Reachability)

---

## 5. Robustness and resistance to errors

### 5.1 Principle of "suffering-focused safe AI" (fail-safe)

- It is easier to strive to avoid catastrophic triggers than to ensure that everything always works correctly.
- Design **graceful or "benign" failures**: failures should be less severe than they could be.

### 5.2 Organizational measures of the provider

- Provide alert techniques with automatic shutdown when robustness is compromised
- Introduce **fail-safe protocols**: predict catastrophic incidents and plan their prevention
- Involve multidisciplinary domain profiles in the design

### 5.3 Technical measures

- Prior dialogues with different stakeholders, maximizing diversity and inclusion
- Model design evaluation committees with experts from diverse backgrounds
- Consider the **possibility of missing information** as a potential error and create alternative mechanisms (alternative lists, incomplete data alerts)

### 5.4 Deployer responsibilities

- Familiarize with fail-safe concepts and implementations
- Use the channels provided by the provider to report errors
- Act upon lack of robustness as a concept of model degradation

---

## 6. Redundancy and backup plans

The provider must supply:

1. **Automatic data backup:** Redundancy of models, algorithms, logs, data, and model executions (MLOps/DevOps)
2. **Fail-safe mechanisms** for components throughout the lifecycle
3. **Action plan** for effective recovery of data, models, algorithms, and the AI system after a failure
4. **Reproducibility tools:** Tracing, provenance, and data lineage to detect modifications that affect robustness or performance

Recommended techniques (MLOps):
- Geographic redundancy
- Scalable systems with capacity based on demand
- Backup systems and data version tracking
- DevOps platforms with operations automation, centralized computing, continuous error monitoring, and container technology

The **deployer** must be aware of redundancy measures, backup plans, and failure tests, and replicate installation and maintenance instructions.

---

## 7. Robustness for systems that continue learning

### 7.1 Organizational requirements

- Experts covering degradation monitoring at all stages (data scientist, data engineer, MLOps/DevOps)
- Continuous learning strategies and deep learning model compression from the design phase
- Ensure that continuous training does not deteriorate KPIs or robustness metrics
- **Annotate human feedback** so that future model versions take it into account (HITL/HOTL paradigm)

### 7.2 Three types of model degradation

| Type | Description | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Model bias** | Difference between average predictions and actual values. Systematic error (underfitting). | Increase model complexity or improve quality/quantity of training data |
| **Model drift** | Predictions degrade due to changes in the environment. P(Y\|X) changes. | Battery of tests to detect silent failures, monitor impact of data changes on accuracy |
| **Data drift** | Input data changes its distribution P(X). Domain shift. | Domain adaptation techniques, transfer learning |

### 7.3 Mitigation strategies

- Network retraining (when feasible)
- Dataset shift strategies, out-of-distribution (OOD), zero-shot generalization
- Continual learning: **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)**, Piggyback, **Experience Replay** (replay buffer, prototype, rehearsal)
- **Domain adaptation** and **transfer learning** for data shift
- Detection of **causal relationships** as a source of bias: Neural Causation Coefficient (NCC), classifiers to detect selection bias
- When ground truth is not available: **CBPE** (Confidence-based Performance Estimation) for classification, **DLE** (Direct Loss Estimation) for regression
- For **catastrophic forgetting**: continual learning metrics — backward transfer, forward transfer, model size efficiency, samples storage size efficiency, computational efficiency

## 8. Technical documentation

Robustness documentation must contain at minimum:

- Instructions on how to obtain and interpret robustness measures and metrics
- Possible ranges of configurable parameters, input/output data ranges, estimated latency and efficiency measures
- Minimum acceptable values for robustness metrics guaranteed by the provider
- Updates after each relevant model modification, retraining, or change in operational context
- Explanation of challenges and **trade-offs** between responsible AI principles (e.g., global fairness vs. individual privacy, robustness vs. privacy)
- **Responsible AI scorecard** along with dataset cards and model cards

### 8.1 Certification of model robustness

Licenses and certifications considered:
- Hippocratic License
- RAIL (Responsible AI Licenses)
- Ethicas foundation (certification/seal)

Examples of techniques for certifying robustness:
- **Stability Training** for image models against distortions
- **TextAttack** to evaluate and strengthen NLP models with adversarial training
- Principled adversarial training with Lagrange penalty formulation perturbing the data distribution in a Wasserstein ball

Certification phases:
1. **Operation and monitoring:** Evaluate robustness in the operational environment, alert the operator, activate safety modes. Use counterfactual data augmentation, plausible counterfactuals, ontologies to explain data drift.
2. **Reassessment:** Monitoring of V&V tests in accordance with EU ethical principles, through regularly updated licenses.

## 9. Metrics and tests for AI systems (Annex I)

### 9.1 Metrics for establishing robustness

**Multiclass classification:**
- Cohen's Kappa coefficient, Confusion matrix and derived metrics, Hinge Loss, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

**Binary classification:**
- Precision-Recall curve, ROC curve, Balanced accuracy

**Multi-label classification:**
- F1 score

**Scoring systems:**
- AUC, Lift

**Statistical methods:**
- RMSE, MaxError, Correlation between actual and predicted values, Cross-validation (K-fold, Group K-fold, Leave-One-Out, Monte Carlo)

**Robustness associated with model accuracy:**
- AUROC (with caution on imbalanced data), Cumulative Response Curve (CRC) / Gain Curve, Hamming Loss, 5x2 cross-validation schemes

### 9.2 Testing methods

- **Formal methods:** Mathematical proofs over a domain (uncertainty analysis, SMT optimizers, Branch and Bound, constraint programming, Maximum stable space property)
- **Empirical methods:** Experimentation, observation, expert judgment, field trials (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-3:2021)
- **Input perturbation tests:** Evaluate behavior under perturbations in production (image: vibration, rotation, brightness, noise; sound: frequency ranges; RL: disturbing forces)

### 9.3 Formal methods and their applicability

**Types of formal verifiers:**
- Complete vs. incomplete
- Deterministic vs. non-deterministic
- White-box vs. black-box
- With or without computer arithmetic

**Neural network verification:**
- PLNN (Piecewise Linear Neural Networks): with SMT solver or MILP
- BNN (Binarized Neural Nets): with Boolean satisfiability (ILP)
- Solvers (MILP, SMT): Deterministic and white-box
- Abstract Interpretation Verifiers: For large and complex systems, with a trade-off between precision and scalability

**Specific models:**
- **Transformers:** Layer-wise decomposition, verification in each sublayer
- **RNNs:** Model checking and abstract interpretation
- **Reinforcement learning:** Testing in simulation and real world, metrics adapted to task and context

## 10. Robustness and uncertainty (Annex II)

### 10.1 Two main types of uncertainty

| Type | Description | Reducibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Aleatoric** | Inherent to the stochastic nature of the dependence between instances x and y | Not reducible |
| **Epistemic** | Due to limitations in the model's knowledge | Reducible by collecting more data |

The provider must monitor and report throughout the entire lifecycle:
1. Changes in the distribution of training and test data (data shift)
2. Disparities in domain or machine learning task shift
3. Changes in new data acquisition mechanisms (artifacts, biases)
4. Catastrophic forgetting and inability to learn continuously

### 10.2 Methods for quantifying uncertainty

- Classification model calibration: isotonic regression, **Sigmoid / Platt scaling**
- Indirect uncertainty assessment: **accuracy-rejection curves**
- Calibration measures: Macro-average accuracy, proportion of classes, MSE, LogLoss, CalBin, CalLoss, Avg error, relative error, Anderson-Darling (A2) test

**Tools:**
- IBM Uncertainty Quantification 360 Toolkit
- NannyML (detection of data and model drift in production)

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **Robustness is a design requirement from the start, not an emergent attribute:** High-risk AI systems must be designed to minimize harmful behaviors, detect when they operate outside their domain, and interrupt their operation in a controlled manner when robustness cannot be guaranteed (Recital 75, Art. 15.4).

2. **Five desirable properties define robustness:** Reliability (consistency of estimates), stability (controlled change in response to input variations), sensitivity (bounded output variation), relevance (magnitude of input impact), and reachability (multi-step accuracy in interaction with the environment). Their evaluation must cover both local and global robustness.

3. **Verification and validation as complementary mandatory processes:** Verification confirms that the system meets specifications without real data; validation confirms that it fulfills its intended purpose with real data. Data processed by different architectures may be analyzed with at least one formal method (ISO/IEC 24029-2:2023).

4. **Efficiency and performance are dimensions of robustness:** Robustness metrics must be validated in hardware environments that exactly replicate the final computational capabilities. Techniques such as distillation, edge learning, compression, and network pruning are necessary when resource constraints exist.

5. **Principle of "suffering-focused safe AI" (fail-safe):** It is preferable to design for safe failure than to attempt to guarantee that everything always works correctly. Failures should be "graceful" or "benign," and the system must include automatic shutdown, fail-safe protocols, and alerts when robustness is compromised.

6. **Technical redundancy as a key mechanism:** The provider must implement automatic data backup, fail-safe mechanisms, action plans for recovery, and reproducibility tools (tracing, provenance, data lineage). Geographic redundancy, scalable systems, DevOps/MLOps, and container technology are recommended.

7. **Three types of degradation to monitor:** Model bias (bias/underfitting), model drift (model drift — P(Y|X) changes), and data drift (data drift — P(X) changes). Each type requires specific detection and mitigation strategies, from retraining to domain adaptation and continual learning.

8. **Systems that continue learning require special treatment:** Art. 15.4 requires measures to eliminate or reduce the risk of biased feedback loops. Human feedback must be formally annotated. Techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), Experience Replay, and Piggyback help mitigate catastrophic forgetting.

9. **Formal methods for verifying neural network robustness:** Specific verifiers are available by network type (PLNN with SMT/MILP, BNN with ILP, Transformers by layer decomposition, RNNs with model checking). Verifiers can be complete or incomplete, white-box or black-box, with a trade-off between precision and scalability.

10. **Two types of uncertainty with different treatment:** Aleatoric uncertainty (irreducible) and epistemic uncertainty (reducible with more data). The provider must monitor changes in data distribution, domain disparities, changes in data acquisition, and catastrophic forgetting, using tools such as IBM UQ 360 and NannyML.

11. **Documentation of trade-offs between AI principles:** The model card must make explicit the minimum expected guarantees and the trade-offs that have been tested (e.g., global fairness vs. individual privacy, robustness vs. privacy), along with the Responsible AI scorecard.

12. **Robustness certification with specific licenses and techniques:** Before and during operation, it is recommended to use licenses such as Hippocratic License or RAIL, and techniques such as Stability Training (image), TextAttack (NLP), and principled adversarial training with Lagrange-Wasserstein formulation.
# Guide 11. Cybersecurity

**Source:** Spain's AI Sandbox — European Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Reg. 2024/1689)
**Version:** December 10, 2025
**Nature:** Non-binding. Practical recommendations.
**Key technical standards:** ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (ISMS), ISO/IEC 27017:2015 (cloud security controls)
**Core article:** Art. 15 AIA — paragraphs 1 and 5 (cybersecurity)
**Illustrative use cases:** Automated benefits granting system, Work assistance (biometrics)

---

## 1. Concept of cybersecurity for AI

Cybersecurity in high-risk AI systems **is not optional**. These systems are exposed to specific threats that require rigorous protective measures adapted to their context:

- Manipulation of training data that compromises model integrity.
- Attacks designed to force errors in results (adversarial examples).
- Attacks aimed at obtaining private information from training data.
- Model defects or integration errors exploitable by third parties.

AI systems **add their own vulnerabilities** to the general cybersecurity threats of any computer system. Failing to account for them is **inadmissible in a high-risk context**.

**Training** in cybersecurity is fundamental: "a chain is only as strong as its weakest link."

### Main threats

| Type of attack | Phase | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Data poisoning** | Design/Training | Introduction of malicious data into the training set to alter learning or insert backdoors |
| **Model poisoning** | Training/Update | Direct manipulation of model parameters (especially in federated learning) |
| **Evasion attacks** | Inference (production) | The adversary attempts to make the system produce incorrect predictions in specific cases |
| **Inversion attacks** | Inference (production) | Objective: obtain knowledge of the model's training data (Membership Inference, Property Inference) |
| **Extraction attacks** | Inference (production) | Obtain information about the model to replicate it; facilitate subsequent transfer attacks |
| **Side-channel attacks** | Training/Inference | Obtain information through observable physical characteristics (energy consumption, response times, electromagnetic emissions) |
| **Supply chain attacks** | Entire lifecycle | Compromise integrity via compromised software dependencies, malicious libraries, or hardware manipulation |
| **Denial of Service (DoS)** | Inference (production) | Computational overload or resource exhaustion to disrupt operation |

Attacks are classified by the attacker's level of knowledge: **black box** (inputs/outputs only), **white box** (full access to architecture, data, parameters), and **gray box** (a mix of both).

## 3. Cybersecurity level and consistency (Art. 15.1)

High-risk AI systems must achieve an **adequate level** of cybersecurity and operate **consistently throughout their entire lifecycle**.

### AI system lifecycle

Conception → Design → Implementation → Verification and testing → Deployment → Post-market surveillance → Retirement.

### Provider measures

**Organizational:**
- Plan the cybersecurity level globally during design and development.
- Involve the **Data Protection Officer (DPO)** from the design phase.
- Instructions for use must include high-level cybersecurity recommendations, with a read confirmation mechanism.
- Establish a **dashboard** with cybersecurity indicators for AI and minimum frequency of security audits.
- If the system is offered as MLSaaS, all measures are the provider's responsibility.
- For on-premise/in-cloud systems, provide adequate protection instructions, with automatic or semi-automatic installation scripts.

**Technical:**
- Include information on cybersecurity risks in installation processes and instruction manuals.
- Use tools that automate **security testing** (security-oriented design).
- System updates must apply all cybersecurity measures described in the guide.

### Deployer measures

- Distribute installation and configuration information within their organization.
- If the system is on their premises, establish within their **general cybersecurity policy** all AI system assets (data, model, processes, tools, artifacts).
- Maintain a centralized cybersecurity information repository.

---

## 4. Systems resistant to usage alterations (Art. 15.5)

### Asset and actor inventory

The provider must conduct an **asset inventory** and identify the **actors** that interact with them. This inventory enables:

- Establishing the basis for protecting the system from unauthorized third parties.
- Applying **access policies** (see Annex I).
- Sizing cybersecurity technical solutions based on the identified assets.

**Provider measures:**
- Inventory all involved actors (internal and external with inadequate roles).
- Establish the scope and permissions level of each actor.
- Document roles in installation instructions with actor-element detail.
- Identify vulnerabilities across the asset inventory.
- Include a **threat/vulnerability list** in the system instructions.
- Develop a rigorous **threat model** with a **RACI** scheme (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed).
- Adequate IT support for the inventory; consider concurrent access and seasonality.

**Deployer measures:**
- Integrate the actor documentation with their internal organizational chart.
- Establish substitution mechanisms to avoid discontinuities.
- If the system is on their premises, treat it as a cybersecurity asset.

---

## 5. Vulnerabilities and security controls for data (Art. 15.5)

Protection against **poisoning attacks**. Focus is on the **design and training** phase where the risk is very high.

### Vulnerabilities and controls (training data)

| Vulnerability | Security Controls |
|---|---|
| Model easy to poison | **Bagging, boosting, TRIM algorithm** techniques; defined initial seeds; periodic audits (ISO27001/2 - NIST 800-53) |
| Insufficient data for poisoning resistance | **Data augmentation** techniques |
| Inadequate access rights management | **Access policies** (see Annex I) |
| Uncontrolled data sources | Version control; data encrypted in transit and at rest; **DLP** (Data Loss Prevention) tools |
| Lack of control to detect poisoning | **STRIP** technique (STRong Intentional Perturbation): perturb data in a controlled manner and observe variance |
| Failure to detect poisoned samples in the training set | Data sanitization (Z-score, **Local Outlier Factor**, **Isolation Forest**); certify distribution by marginals; analyze impact with **RONI** (Reject on Negative Impact) or **tRONI** |
| Use of incorrect data labeling source | Interconnection management (ISO27001/2); **Label Flipping** protection techniques via k-NN |

Recommended resources: **OWASP Machine Learning Security Top Ten**, **OWASP Top 10: LLM & Generative AI Security Risks**, **OWASP AI Top Ten**.

---

## 6. Protection against adversarial attacks (Art. 15.5)

The system is most exposed during the **inference** phase in **production**. Oracle-type attacks: evasion, inversion, extraction.

### Vulnerabilities and controls (adversarial attacks)

| Vulnerability | Security Controls |
|---|---|
| Transfer of adversarial attacks | Avoid widely known models with known vulnerabilities; **obfuscation or masking** techniques; proactively evaluate robustness against transfer |
| Inadequate model access management | Access policies (Annex I) |
| Lack of consideration of possible attacks | Integration into awareness strategy (Annex II); state-of-the-art monitoring via NIST |
| Absence of a security process | Periodic audits (ISO27001/2 - NIST 800-53); **blue team/red team** approach (white box, gray box, black box) |
| Lack of anomalous input detection | **Adversary detection subnetworks**; confidence metrics and thresholds; **OoD** (Out of Domain data) detection; call origin control; visual alerts to the user. Tools: **IBM ART**, **Foolbox**, **TensorFlow Privacy**, PyTorch libraries |
| Failure to consider evasion attacks | **Input gradient regularization**, **defensive distillation**, input randomization |
| Failure to consider adversarial attacks (misclassification) | Consult **MITRE ATLAS**; **HCNN** (Highly Confident Near Neighbor) framework; **Attribution-driven Causal Analysis** |
| Source/target misclassification | **Feature Denoising**; training with adversarial examples; **Feature Squeezing** |
| Confidence reduction | Input control; restriction by format, IP, origin |
| Use of known models facilitating transfer | Select models with lower transfer risk; do not reuse models without verification and validation |
| Input data controlled by the attacker | Preprocessing (normalization, noise removal); **CAT-Gen** for NLP; input history to detect suspicious proximity; access policies |
| Membership inference attacks | **Differential privacy**; private datasets; **Neural Dropout**; **Model Stacking** |
| Property Inference Attacks | Origin-based limits (IP, user, device); alarm and blocking mechanisms; validation of all inputs |
| Failure to consider extraction attacks | Control of returned information; limit queries; add statistical noise to model output |
| Model theft | Model version control; protection against exposure |

---

## 7. Attacks on AI system defects (Art. 15.5)

Two levels of defects:

- **Model-intrinsic defects:** Vulnerabilities intrinsic to the design, training, or architecture (incomplete data, biases, inappropriate hyperparameter configurations).
- **Model integration defects:** Incorrect configurations, insecure dependencies, failures in the implementation and deployment of the software/hardware ecosystem.

### Vulnerabilities and controls (model defects)

| Vulnerability | Security Controls |
|---|---|
| Model-intrinsic defects | Know and investigate defects of the model family (CNN, RandomForest, KNN, etc.); inventory in the threat model |
| Existence of biases | Refer to data and accuracy guides |
| Compromise of the model framework | Updated frameworks; monitor CVE/CWE; **OWASP Dependency-check**, **Dependency-Track** tools |
| Existence of backdoors | Periodic **SAST** (Static Application Security Testing) and **DAST** (Dynamic Application Security Testing) analyses; **penetration testing** oriented to AI. Tools: **MLSecTools**, **IBM ART** |
| Exposure and access to model code | Access policies with MFA, certificates; detailed logs; protection against exfiltration (no USBs, no public networks); encrypted channels (VPN) |
| Unknown vulnerabilities | Continuous analysis of cybersecurity dashboard indicators |

### Vulnerabilities and controls (integration/configuration defects)

| Vulnerability | Security Controls |
|---|---|
| The system allows extraction of private information | **Differential privacy** mechanism; design with **PATE** (Private Aggregation of Teacher Ensembles) |
| Use of vulnerable components | Component inventory; CVE/CWE tracking; automated tools; management of obsolete components |
| Mismatch between development/testing/production environments | **Container** descriptors (Docker); identical hardware when possible (IoT, edge computing) |
| No correct operation indicators defined | Dashboard/information panel accessible with accuracy, robustness, data distribution metrics, alert thresholds |
| Third-party contracts without adequate security level | Security clauses in contracts: periodic audits, certifications (ISO 27001, ISO 27017), SAST/DAST testing, contingency plans |
| Unknown configuration/integration vulnerabilities | Real-time AI-based monitoring; periodic adversarial attack simulations |

## 9. Annex I: Access policies

Access policies are the **first line of defense** for the cybersecurity of AI systems.

**For the provider:**
- **RBAC** (Role-Based Access Control) paradigm.
- **Least privilege** approach.
- Robust authentication mechanism: password rotation, certificate-based access, **multi-factor authentication (MFA)**.
- **Access revocation** as part of personnel offboarding processes.
- Compatibility with identity management solutions (Active Directory or equivalent).

**Actor groups:** Actors on data, on system configuration/operation, integration developers (MLOps), system administrators, actors on system software, actors on architecture/administration/maintenance, and general personnel (least privileges).

**Data actions to control:** Incorporation, deletion, editing, utilization.

**Model actions to control:** Code access, code editing, utilization for training, training process, validation/deployment to production, access to technical documentation.

**For the deployer:**
- Never allow anonymous access to the high-risk AI system.
- Least privileges, password rotation.
- Access logged and audited periodically.
- Integration with the organization's identity mechanism.

## Key points for evaluating and maintaining AI projects

1. **AI cybersecurity is specific and adds to general cybersecurity:** High-risk AI systems have their own vulnerabilities (poisoning, adversarial attacks, extraction, inversion) that are added to general cybersecurity threats. Failing to consider these specific threats is inadmissible in high-risk contexts.

2. **Seven types of threats throughout the entire lifecycle:** Data poisoning, model poisoning, evasion attacks, inversion attacks, extraction attacks, side-channel attacks, supply chain attacks, and denial of service. Each type has its phase of greatest exposure and requires specific controls.

3. **Asset and actor inventory with RACI scheme:** The provider must inventory all assets (data, model, processes, tools, artifacts) and actors (internal and external), establishing a RACI matrix for each vulnerability identification. This is the basis for sizing cybersecurity solutions.

4. **Access policies as the first line of defense:** RBAC (Role-Based Access Control), least privileges, multi-factor authentication (MFA), password rotation, access revocation upon leaving the organization. These apply to both the provider and the deployer, covering access to data, model, code, and documentation.

5. **Three differentiated vulnerability-control tables:** The guide provides detailed tables of vulnerabilities and security controls for (a) data poisoning attacks, (b) adversarial attacks on the model, and (c) intrinsic model defects and integration defects. Each with specific technical controls.

6. **Blue team/red team as a security approach:** The blue team develops the model and the red team subjects it to adversarial attack testing (evasion, inversion, extraction) with various levels of access (white box, gray box, black box). Automated tests must be executed with each release and when incorporating new data.

7. **AI-specific security tools:** IBM ART, Foolbox, MLSecTools for adversarial robustness testing; STRIP and TRIM for poisoning detection; OWASP Dependency-check for dependency vulnerabilities; periodic SAST/DAST; penetration testing oriented to AI.

8. **Feedback loops are a cybersecurity risk:** In continuous training systems, output results can influence input data for future operations, amplifying biases or undesirable behaviors. This must be monitored as a cybersecurity risk.

9. **Mandatory and role-differentiated training:** Both the provider and the deployer must train their staff in AI-specific cybersecurity, covering poisoning, adversarial attacks, model defects, RBAC, and MFA. Training must be updated as threats evolve.

10. **The deployer inherits provider responsibilities if they train the model:** If the deployer performs final training or uses continuous training, all provider cybersecurity measures regarding data (organizational and technical) apply to them.

11. **Cybersecurity indicator dashboard with alert thresholds:** The system must have an accessible panel with accuracy, robustness, data distribution metrics, and clearly defined thresholds that generate alerts upon anomalies, facilitating early detection of attacks or degradations.

12. **Technical cybersecurity documentation structured by domains:** The cybersecurity plan, the asset and actor inventory, and the three vulnerability-control tables (poisoning, adversarial attacks, model defects) must be documented, along with the prevention and detection measures implemented.
# Guide 12. Records and Automatically Generated Log Files

- **Source:** 12-guia-de-registros.pdf (32 pages)
- **Version:** December 10, 2025
- **Character:** Practical guide for providers and deployers of high-risk AI systems (HRAIS) on generating, conserving, and managing logs/records throughout the system lifecycle
- **Key standards:** ISO 15489-1:2016 (Records Management), ISO 31000:2018 (Risk Management), ISO/IEC 23894 (AI Risk Management), ISO/IEC 42001 (AI Management System), prEN 18229-1 (AI Trustworthiness - Logging), prEN ISO/IEC 24970 (AI System Logging)
- **Articles:** Art. 12 (Conservation of Records), Art. 19 (Automatically Generated Log Files), Art. 26.6 (Deployer Obligations)
- **Use cases:** AI system for employee promotion and advancement, AI insulin administration system, biometric attendance recognition system

---

## 1. What is a Record (Log) in AI?

A **record** is a file that stores information about the behavior and performance of an AI system during training or production use. It can include input data, predictions, errors, and anomalies.

Records serve as:
- Tools for **analysis and continuous improvement** (understanding behavior, optimizing precision/efficiency)
- **Evidence assets** for tracking execution and ensuring the system operates as intended
- Instruments for **detecting errors or biases**

A **records management system** is the set of processes to collect, store, analyze, and manage records, monitoring performance, detecting problems, and ensuring security/privacy compliance.

---

## 2. Principles for Records Management

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Protect privacy, prevent unauthorized disclosure or access |
| **Integrity** | Records must be precise, complete, without unauthorized changes |
| **Availability** | Records accessible when needed for analysis, audit, or review |
| **Authenticity** | Records registered legitimately, not manipulated by unauthorized users |
| **Accessibility and traceability** | Available for review with traceability information to identify source and origin |
| **Responsibility** | Records are the AI system owner's responsibility, treated adequately and securely |
| **Retention and elimination** | Retained for an adequate period, then securely eliminated to comply with data protection regulations |

---

## 3. Regulatory Articles

### Art. 12 - Conservation of Records

- **12.1:** HRAIS must technically permit **automatic recording of events** (log files) throughout the **entire system lifecycle**
- **12.2:** To ensure traceability, logging capabilities must record relevant events for:
  - a) **Detection of risk situations** (Art. 79.1) or substantial modifications
  - b) **Facilitation of post-market surveillance** (Art. 72)
  - c) **Monitoring of system functioning** by deployers (Art. 26.5)
- **12.3:** For Annex III, point 1(a) systems (biometric identification), records must include at minimum:
  - a) Period of each use (start/end date and time)
  - b) Reference database used for input data matching
  - c) Input data that produced a match
  - d) Identification of natural persons involved in results verification (Art. 14.5)

### Art. 19 - Automatically Generated Log Files

- Providers must **conserve log files** that HRAIS generate automatically, as long as files are **under their control**
- Retention period: **at least 6 months** (unless EU/national law, especially data protection, requires otherwise)
- **Financial entities** must maintain logs as part of documentation under financial services law

### Art. 26.6 - Deployer Obligations

- Deployers must conserve automatically generated log files **under their control** for at least **6 months**
- Financial entity deployers must maintain logs as part of financial services documentation

## 7. Records Management System - Four Core Processes

### 7.1 Evaluation and Design of Records

The analysis process to determine the **need** to generate records and what **information** to collect. Five steps:

1. **Identification of need** - Why is the record needed? What are the objectives?
2. **Identification of objectives** - What results are expected? Clear and specific goals
3. **Definition of scope** - What information? How long retained? Avoid unnecessary data
4. **Design of the record** - Define fields, categories, tools, and software for capture/storage/access
5. **Identification of responsible persons** - Who collects, maintains, and ensures integrity?

Three evaluation contexts for determining what to record:

| Context | Focus | Key Article |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk situations** (5.1.1) | Events that could cause the HRAIS to present a risk to health, safety, or fundamental rights | Art. 12.2.a / Art. 79.1 |
| **Post-market surveillance** (5.1.2) | Data needed after commercialization; active and systematic collection and analysis | Art. 12.2.b / Art. 72.2 |
| **Human oversight** (5.1.3) | Information the system must provide to fulfill human oversight needs; identifying serious incidents to inform provider and interrupt operation | Art. 12.2.c / Art. 26.5 |

### 7.2 Capture, Storage, and Access Control

Capturing, storing, and conserving defined records while **guaranteeing protection** against unauthorized access, unwanted modifications, losses, or destruction:

- a) Collect information per design-phase criteria
- b) Select adequate **storage media** with protection (e.g., redundant servers)
- c) Implement **cybersecurity and access control** measures
- d) Define **roles and responsibilities** for record management
- e) Ensure adequate **training** for personnel (storage, cybersecurity, access control)
- f) Consider applicable regulations (**GDPR**, data protection laws)
- g) **Periodically review** storage media, cybersecurity, and access control measures

Records must include access metadata: user ID, permission level, geographic location, date/time of access.

### 7.3 Retention and Elimination

Establishing conservation and destruction needs, governed by two factors:
- a) **Evaluation needs** - retain records needed for ongoing risk assessment and compliance verification
- b) **Regulatory requirements** - GDPR, data protection laws, minimum 6-month retention per AI Act

Destruction must be **authorized, documented**, and comply with security/access measures. Records involved in legal proceedings cannot be destroyed until authorized.

### 7.4 Monitoring and Continuous Improvement

Five phases in the improvement cycle:
1. **Monitoring and error identification** - detect errors in data, performance, security
2. **Analysis of recorded data** - determine root causes, find patterns and correlations
3. **Implementation of improvements** - update software, procedures, or personnel training
4. **Evaluation of improvements** - compare before/after data
5. **Continuous improvement cycle** - repeat to maintain and improve system performance

## Key Points for Evaluating and Maintaining AI Projects

1. **Automatic logging is mandatory:** HRAIS must technically permit automatic event recording throughout the entire system lifecycle - this is a fundamental Art. 12 requirement
2. **Minimum 6-month retention:** Both providers and deployers must conserve automatically generated log files for at least 6 months, unless data protection or other law requires otherwise
3. **Three mandatory logging purposes:** Records must enable (a) risk situation detection, (b) post-market surveillance facilitation, and (c) human oversight/monitoring of system functioning
4. **Seven principles govern records:** Confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity, accessibility/traceability, responsibility, and retention/elimination must all be guaranteed
5. **Biometric systems have enhanced requirements:** Remote biometric identification systems (Annex III, 1.a) must log usage periods, reference databases, input data, and identification of 2+ persons verifying results
6. **Four-process management system:** Implement evaluation/design, capture/storage/access control, retention/elimination, and monitoring/continuous improvement as an integrated system
7. **Evaluation before capture:** Always evaluate the need, objectives, scope, and design of records before building capture mechanisms - align records to risk management, post-market surveillance, and human oversight needs
8. **Security and access control are essential:** Records must be protected against unauthorized access, modification, loss, or destruction, with cybersecurity measures, GDPR compliance, and periodic reviews
9. **Assign clear responsibilities:** Define and document specific roles for each records management process (evaluation, design, capture, operations, storage, security, training, retention/elimination, overall oversight)
10. **Financial entities have additional obligations:** Must maintain AI log files as part of documentation conserved under EU financial services legislation
11. **Technical documentation is cross-cutting:** Records management should be integrated into every section of Annex IV technical documentation, not just the two directly related points (2(g) and 9)
12. **Continuous improvement is required:** Implement a monitoring cycle that identifies errors, analyzes data, implements improvements, evaluates effectiveness, and repeats continuously
# Guide 13. Post-Market Surveillance Plan

- **Source:** 13-guia-vigilancia-poscomercializacion.pdf (34 pages)
- **Version:** December 10, 2025
- **Character:** Practical guide for providers and deployers of high-risk AI systems (HRAIS) on implementing post-market surveillance systems and plans
- **Key standards:** ISO/IEC 25059:2021 (SQuaRE - Quality model for AI systems), ISO/IEC 25000:2021 (SQuaRE - Systems and software quality)
- **Articles:** Art. 72 (Post-market Surveillance by Providers and Post-market Surveillance Plan for HRAIS)
- **Use cases:** AI system for employee promotion and advancement, AI insulin administration system (smart insulin pump)

---

## 1. What is a Post-Market Surveillance System?

A **post-market surveillance system** for HRAIS is a set of **processes and tools** oriented to collecting data from a system and transforming them into indicators about its activity, with the objective of supervising AI systems **after their market launch**.

The goal is to enable the provider to evaluate whether the AI system continues to meet the requirements established in Chapter III, Section 2 ("Requirements of High-Risk Systems") throughout its entire lifecycle.

### Four Subsystems

| Subsystem | Function |
|---|---|
| **Indicator capture systems** | Processes that collect data on system performance, infrastructure, user interactions, and security |
| **Indicator registration systems** | Storage services conforming to the Records Guide (Guide 12) measures |
| **Automated alert system** | Monitors indicator changes against preset scales to alert on possible risk scenarios |
| **Analysis interfaces for supervisors** | Access points for surveillance personnel to analyze extracted indicators (web apps, raw data listings, dashboards) |

---

## 2. Why is Post-Market Surveillance Needed?

Post-market surveillance includes:
- Data collection on **performance and security**
- Evaluation of **possible causes** of detected problems
- Implementation of **solutions** to correct problems
- **Communication** of results and recommendations to stakeholders
- Implementation of **additional security controls** against malicious attacks
- Periodic evaluations ensuring ongoing compliance with all AI Act requirements (data governance, technical documentation, records, human oversight, transparency, precision, robustness, cybersecurity)

### Entities Subject to Sector-Specific Legislation

Providers subject to legislative acts in Annex I, Section A who have already implemented a conforming surveillance system and plan may **integrate** the required elements into their existing systems and plans, provided an equivalent level of protection is achieved. This also applies to Annex III, point 5 systems deployed by **financial entities**.

---

## 3. Regulatory Article

### Art. 72 - Post-Market Surveillance by Providers

- **72.1:** Providers shall establish and document a post-market surveillance system **proportionate** to the nature of the AI technologies and the risks of the HRAIS
- **72.2:** The system shall actively and systematically collect, document, and analyze relevant data that deployers may provide or that may be collected through other sources on HRAIS functioning throughout its **entire useful life**. When applicable, it shall include analysis of **interaction with other AI systems**. It shall not include sensitive operational data of deployers who are law enforcement authorities
- **72.3:** The system shall be based on a **post-market surveillance plan**. The plan shall form **part of the technical documentation** referred to in Annex IV. The Commission shall adopt an implementing act establishing a **template** for the plan by **2 February 2026**
- **72.4:** For HRAIS regulated by Union harmonization legislative acts in Annex I, Section A, providers may integrate required elements into existing systems/plans under those acts, provided an equivalent protection level is achieved

## 4. System Design Prerequisites

Before designing the surveillance system, the following guides should be completed first:
1. **Risk Management Guide** (Guide 5)

### Four System Components to Implement

1. **Selection of indicators** - Based on the risk management system; the AI system must generate records with associated indicator information
2. **Indicator capture and transmission systems** - Collect data from log files and send to indicator registration systems with secure transmission
3. **Indicator registration system** - A records management system (any structured/unstructured database) with access control, retention, and elimination policies
4. **Alert system and supervisor analysis interface** - Real-time alert system for anomalous values plus a dashboard/interface for manual exploration

## 5. Six Elements of the Surveillance Plan

### 5.1 Continuous Surveillance

Real-time monitoring of AI system performance and behavior in production to **detect and correct abrupt changes**. Especially relevant for:
- **Data aging** (training data becoming stale)
- **Insufficient or inaccurate training data**
- **Malicious attacks** (adversarial learning, phishing, data theft)

Five monitoring techniques:
- **AI system indicators** - Performance and behavior data analysis to detect patterns and trends
- **User action indicators** - How users interact (commands, queries, responses) to detect usability problems
- **Infrastructure indicators** - CPU, memory, storage monitoring for performance/capacity issues
- **Security indicators** - Detecting phishing, brute force attacks, malware
- **Alert-based indicator change supervision** - Rules and thresholds configured for anomalous events

### 5.2 Periodic Surveillance

Regular evaluations with a **longer review cycle** depending on system characteristics. Includes:

- **Performance tests** - Stress tests measuring response time and data handling capacity
- **Precision tests** - Measuring accuracy on specific tasks, using metrics from the Precision Guide (Guide 9)
- **Historical results tracking** - Maintaining a test history to discover early performance degradation trends
- **Control sample comparison** - Maintaining a manual control sample to compare against AI system decisions (e.g., keeping some employees evaluated manually vs. by the AI promotion system)
- **Watch for metric gaming** - When the system's own decisions improve its metrics indirectly (e.g., promotion system retraining on its own outcomes creates self-reinforcing bias)

### 5.3 Incident Reports

System for collecting and analyzing incident reports. Five steps:

1. **Define system objectives** - What information to collect and how to use it
2. **System design** - Define incident collection/analysis processes, report structure, and data analysis mechanisms
3. **System implementation** - Develop platform for collecting/storing reports and analysis tools
4. **Training** - Train end users, administrators, and data analysis teams
5. **Continuous monitoring** - Collect user feedback, review reports, analyze trends

Incident report content should include:
- System characteristics and objectives
- Anomalous behavior detected (indicator, context, number of affected systems, impact)
- Actions taken so far
- Configuration and system adjustments at the time of the anomaly
- Historical extract of registers and indicators
- Supervisor notes

### 5.4 Transparent Communication

Adapt communication of system characteristics, performance, and consequences to the **recipient's level of understanding**:

- Establish **clear and quantifiable** performance and security indicators
- Collect and analyze performance/security data
- Provide clear information through **periodic reports, tracking dashboards, or dedicated websites**
- Explain system functioning including algorithms, data used, and how decisions are made
- Translate technical metrics into understandable language (e.g., "96% precision" should be explained as "of 100 employees the system indicated should be promoted, it was correct for 96 and made errors for 4")

### 5.5 Training

Supervisors must receive training on:
- Context of system use and consequences of predictions
- Data considered for predictions and how external factors may affect them
- Algorithm comprehension and generalization methods
- Practical experience with **anomalous behavior scenarios** (failed predictions, infrastructure overload)
- End users (e.g., HR specialists) should also be trained to detect and report anomalous situations

### 5.6 Flexibility

The plan must be **flexible and scalable** to adapt to changes. Eight measures:

| Measure | Description |
|---|---|
| a) Identify applicable regulations | Sector-specific and data protection regulations |
| b) Evaluate performance and security | Use clear, quantifiable indicators |
| c) Identify risks | Performance and security risks |
| d) Establish risk prevention procedures | Incident communication, investigation, and solution implementation |
| e) Monitor regulatory compliance | Sector-specific and data protection regulations |
| f) Establish contingency plan | Prevent critical incidents or failures |
| g) Implement continuous review system | Evaluate performance/security, identify problems, take measures |
| h) Specific training on existing regulations | AI regulations, data protection, and AI ethics best practices |

## 8. Annex A - Surveillance Indicators

An **indicator** is a data point placed within a scale that allows measuring its impact on one or more concrete consequences. Two elements define an indicator:
1. **Scale** - The minimum and maximum acceptable values
2. **Impact** - What consequences the current measurement has

### A.I - AI System Indicators

| Indicator | Data | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Predictions per time unit | Number of predictions made (e.g., 1,567/min) | Min/max expected predictions without performance loss (e.g., 200-22,000) |
| Average prediction time | Time between input and response (e.g., 0.65s) | Min/max acceptable time (e.g., 0.2s-2.5s) |
| Processing queue | Tasks waiting to be processed (e.g., 10) | Acceptable queue range (e.g., 0-50) |
| Prediction error rate* | % incorrect predictions (e.g., 3%) | Error tolerance range (e.g., 0%-10%) |
| Model precision* | % correct predictions (e.g., 97%) | Desired precision range (e.g., 85%-100%) |
| Recall (exhaustivity)* | % true positives over total real positives (e.g., 90%) | Acceptable recall range (e.g., 80%-100%) |
| F1 Score* | Harmonic mean of precision and recall (e.g., 0.85) | Acceptable F1 range (e.g., 0.7-1.0) |
| Input statistical variation | Statistical measures (mean, median, std dev) of inputs (e.g., mean 6.8 tokens, std dev 2.1) | Acceptable range per input (e.g., mean 1-100) |

*Error-related metrics may not be obtainable in real time; applicable in periodic surveillance.

### A.II - Infrastructure Indicators

| Indicator | Data | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| CPU usage | % CPU utilization (e.g., 60%) | 20%-90% |
| Average system processes | Processes per time unit (e.g., 433/min) | 53-2,500/min |
| RAM usage | % memory utilization (e.g., 70%) | 30%-95% |
| GPU usage | % GPU utilization (e.g., 55%) | 20%-85% |
| Storage usage | % storage utilization (e.g., 80%) | 10%-90% |
| Uptime | % operational time without interruption (e.g., 26h) | Max 72h |
| Network bandwidth | Data transmission capacity (e.g., 1.62 Gbps) | 100 Mbps-10 Gbps |
| System temperature | Average temperature (e.g., 22C) | 15C-30C |

### A.III - User Action Indicators

| Indicator | Data | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Session starts | Daily session starts (e.g., 500) | 100-1,000 daily |
| Interactions per user per time unit | Predictions per user per period (e.g., 50/day) | 0-1,000/day |
| Average session duration | Session duration (e.g., 15 min) | 5-60 min |
| Interface interactions | User-interface interactions per period (e.g., 2,000/day) | 500-5,000 daily |
| Per-user input statistics | Same as system-level input statistics but per individual user | Same as system-level |
| Session origin diversity | Number of different session origins per user (e.g., 3) | 1-20 per user |

### A.IV - Security Indicators

| Indicator | Data | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Failed login attempts | Failed logins per period (e.g., 50/day) | 0-100 daily |
| Unauthorized file changes | Unauthorized modifications detected (e.g., 1) | High importance from a single change |
| Active users in system | Users logged in simultaneously (e.g., 10) | 2-3 users |
| Network data volume | Data transferred per time unit (e.g., 103.2 MB/s) | 10-750 MB/s |
| Open network connections | Connections per time unit (e.g., 2,498/h) | 200-20,000/h |

Security indicators must be adapted and expanded by security officers based on the cybersecurity guide and combined with manual log analysis.

## Key Points for Evaluating and Maintaining AI Projects

1. **Post-market surveillance is mandatory and proportionate:** Art. 72.1 requires providers to establish a surveillance system proportionate to the AI technology's nature and risks -- not one-size-fits-all
2. **Active and systematic data collection:** The system must actively collect and analyze data throughout the HRAIS's entire useful life, including potential interaction analysis with other AI systems (Art. 72.2)
3. **Surveillance plan is part of technical documentation:** The plan must be included in Annex IV technical documentation, with a Commission template expected by 2 February 2026 (Art. 72.3)
4. **Four subsystems are required:** Implement indicator capture, indicator registration, automated alerts, and supervisor analysis interfaces as an integrated surveillance system
5. **Six plan elements:** The surveillance plan must cover continuous surveillance, periodic surveillance, incident reports, transparent communication, training, and flexibility
6. **Continuous vs. periodic surveillance serve different purposes:** Continuous monitoring detects abrupt real-time changes; periodic evaluations measure longer-term degradation, precision trends, and scalability through scheduled tests
7. **Four categories of indicators must be monitored:** AI system indicators (predictions, errors, precision), infrastructure (CPU, memory, uptime), user actions (sessions, interactions), and security (failed logins, unauthorized changes)
8. **Watch for self-reinforcing bias:** When the AI system retrains on its own outputs, metrics may improve artificially while actual quality degrades -- maintain control samples for comparison
9. **Transparent communication requires audience adaptation:** Technical metrics must be translated into understandable language for each audience (deployers, regulators, stakeholders) rather than reported raw
10. **Prerequisite guides must be completed first:** Risk management, precision, robustness, cybersecurity, records, and human oversight guides should all be developed before designing the surveillance system
11. **Flexibility and contingency planning are essential:** The plan must adapt to regulatory changes, new threats, organizational changes (e.g., mergers), and evolving AI capabilities
12. **Independent evaluation is recommended:** When possible, seek external certification or accredited entity review for objective assessment of performance and security
# Guide 14. Notification of Serious Incidents

- **Source:** 14-guia-gestion-de-incidentes.pdf (25 pages)
- **Version:** December 10, 2025
- **Character:** Practical guide for providers and deployers of high-risk AI systems (HRAIS) on the procedure for notifying serious incidents to market surveillance authorities
- **Articles:** Art. 73 (Notification of Serious Incidents), Art. 3.49 (Definition of Serious Incident), Art. 26.5 (Deployer Obligations), Art. 17.1.i (Quality Management System), Art. 13.3.a (Transparency)
- **Use cases:** AI system for granting economic aid to low-income families, AI insulin administration system (smart insulin pump)

---

## 1. What is a Serious Incident?

A **serious incident** is defined in Art. 3, paragraph 49 of the AI Act. It is an incident or malfunction of an AI system that, directly or indirectly, may result in any of the following consequences:

- a) **Death** of a person or serious harm to their health
- b) A serious and irreversible **disruption to the management and operation of critical infrastructure**
- c) **Breach of EU law obligations** designed to protect fundamental rights
- d) Serious **damage to property or the environment**

The notification process is continuous -- it does not end with a single report but involves ongoing follow-up and review.

---

## 2. Regulatory Article

### Art. 73 - Notification of Serious Incidents

- **73.1:** Providers of HRAIS placed on the EU market shall notify any serious incident to the **market surveillance authorities (AVMs)** of the Member States where the incident occurred
- **73.2:** Notification shall be made **immediately** after the provider establishes a causal link (or reasonable probability of one) between the AI system and the incident, and **no later than 15 days** after the provider or deployer becomes aware of the incident. The deadline shall account for the **magnitude** of the incident
- **73.3:** In case of **widespread infringement** or a serious incident as defined in Art. 3.49(b) (critical infrastructure), notification shall be made **immediately and no later than 2 days** after becoming aware
- **73.4:** In case of **death**, notification shall be made **immediately** after establishing or suspecting a causal link, and **no later than 10 days** after becoming aware
- **73.5:** To ensure timeliness, the provider may submit an **initial incomplete notification** followed by a complete one
- **73.6:** After notification, the provider shall **investigate without delay**, including risk assessment and corrective measures. The provider shall **cooperate** with authorities and the affected notified body, and shall **not modify the AI system** in any way that could affect subsequent cause evaluation without informing authorities first
- **73.7:** Upon receiving a notification for an incident affecting **fundamental rights** (Art. 3.49(c)), the AVM shall inform the relevant national authorities or public bodies referred to in Art. 77.1. The Commission will publish **specific guidance** by 2 August 2025
- **73.8:** The AVM shall adopt **adequate measures within 7 days** per Art. 19 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 (Market Surveillance Regulation), including possible temporary withdrawal or prohibition
- **73.9:** For Annex III HRAIS subject to EU legislative instruments with **equivalent notification obligations**, notification shall be **limited to** incidents in Art. 3.49(c) (fundamental rights breaches)
- **73.10:** For HRAIS that are **safety components of products** or are themselves products regulated by Regulations (EU) 2017/745 and 2017/746 (medical devices), notification shall be **limited to** Art. 3.49(c) incidents and directed to the **national competent authority** designated by the Member State
- **73.11:** National competent authorities shall **immediately notify the Commission** of any serious incident, regardless of whether measures have been taken, per Art. 20 of Regulation (EU) 2019/1020

## 3. Notification Deadlines

The provider (or the deployer, if unable to contact the provider) must notify within the following **maximum** timeframes:

| Incident Type | Maximum Notification Deadline |
|---|---|
| **Critical infrastructure** disruption (Art. 3.49(b)) | **2 days** |
| **Death** (Art. 3.49(a) -- death) | **10 days** |
| **All other serious incidents** under Art. 3.49 | **15 days** |

### Exception Categories

Three categories of HRAIS have limited notification scope -- they only need to report incidents that breach **fundamental rights** (Art. 3.49(c)):

| Exception Category | Regulation |
|---|---|
| Annex III systems subject to EU legislative instruments with equivalent notification obligations | Art. 73.9 |
| Safety components of products, or products themselves, regulated as **medical devices** | Regulation (EU) 2017/745 |
| Safety components of products, or products themselves, regulated as **in vitro diagnostic medical devices** | Regulation (EU) 2017/746 |

### Who Must Notify

- The **provider** is the primary responsible party for notification
- If the incident is detected by the **deployer** and the deployer **cannot contact the provider**, Art. 73 applies *mutatis mutandis* to the deployer (per Art. 26.5)
- Notification must be sent to **all** AVMs of Member States where the incident occurred (the AI system may operate in multiple countries)

---

## 4. Market Surveillance Authority (AVM) Procedure

Upon receiving notification from the provider:

1. **Immediately notify** the European Commission (regardless of measures taken)
2. **Manage within 7 days** per the Market Surveillance Regulation (EU) 2019/1020, Art. 19 -- possible measures include temporary withdrawal or prohibition of the system
3. If the incident involves a **fundamental rights breach** (Art. 3.49(c)), inform the relevant **national authorities or public bodies** responsible for supervising fundamental rights protection (particularly the right to equality and non-discrimination)

---

## 5. Five Measures for Managing Serious Incidents

### 5.1 Frame Incident Management within the QMS

Per Art. 17.1.i, procedures for notifying serious incidents must be embedded in the provider's **Quality Management System (QMS)**. Both provider and deployer should include these procedures in their QMS or AI governance model.

**Applies to:** Provider (primary), Deployer (if unable to contact provider)

### 5.2 Contact with the Market Surveillance Authority

It is essential to have established contact with the AVM, since that is the authority to which incidents must be notified.

**Applies to:**
- Provider (as responsible for notification)
- Deployer (per Art. 26 *mutatis mutandis* obligation if unable to contact provider)

### 5.3 Contact with the Provider

When the deployer detects an incident, they must contact the provider through the **communication channel** established per Art. 13.3.a (Transparency -- provider identity and contact information).

**Applies to:**
- Provider must establish and provide the contact channel
- Deployer must identify the persons in their organization responsible for activating this contact channel when serious incidents occur

### 5.4 Knowledge of AI System Categorization

The first step is identifying whether the AI system belongs to one of the **exception categories** (Annex III with equivalent legislation, medical devices, in vitro diagnostics), because if it does and the incident does **not** affect fundamental rights, notification to the AVM is **not required**.

**Applies to:** Provider and Deployer

**Example -- Aid granting system:** Classified under Annex III, Point 5 (access to essential public and private services). Notification to AVM is only required when it affects EU fundamental rights.

**Example -- Insulin pump:** Classified under Annex II-A, Subsection 11 (medical devices). Not within the exceptions -- any incident must be notified to the AVM since it potentially affects the right to life.

### 5.5 Knowledge of Fundamental Rights

When the system belongs to an exception category, it is essential to know which **EU fundamental rights** apply to determine whether notification is required.

**Applies to:** Provider and Deployer

**Example -- Aid granting system:** If the AI discriminates in granting aid based on sex, race, ethnicity, genetic characteristics, language, religion, political opinions, national minority membership, heritage, disability, age, or sexual orientation, this would breach the fundamental right to **EQUALITY** (Section III of the Charter), requiring incident notification.

**Example -- Insulin pump:** Not in the exceptions, so all incidents must be notified -- any incident potentially affects the fundamental right to **life**.

## 6. Visual Summary of the Full Procedure

### Provider Flow:
1. Serious incident occurs
2. Check: Is the AI system in an exception category?
   - **NO** → Follow detailed instructions (published by 2 August 2025); Notify AVM within max 2 days (critical infrastructure), 10 days (death), or 15 days (other Art. 3.49 incidents); Evaluate impact and implement corrective measures; Cooperate with competent national authorities and affected notified body
   - **YES** → Check: Does it affect fundamental rights?
     - **YES** → Same notification procedure as above
     - **NO** → No AVM notification required (but still evaluate impact and implement corrective measures)

### AVM Flow:
1. Receive notification from provider
2. Immediately notify European Commission
3. Manage within 7 days per Market Surveillance Regulation
4. Check: Does it affect fundamental rights?
   - **YES** → Inform national authorities or public bodies
   
### National Authorities/Public Bodies:
- Manage the fundamental rights aspect of the incident

## Key Points for Evaluating and Maintaining AI Projects

1. **Serious incidents must be notified to market surveillance authorities:** Art. 73.1 requires providers to notify any serious incident to the AVMs of all Member States where the incident occurred -- this is mandatory, not optional
2. **Four categories of serious incidents:** Death or serious health harm, critical infrastructure disruption, breach of fundamental rights obligations, and serious property/environmental damage (Art. 3.49)
3. **Strict notification deadlines apply:** 2 days for critical infrastructure, 10 days for deaths, 15 days for other serious incidents -- starting from when the provider or deployer becomes aware
4. **Deployers inherit notification duties when provider is unreachable:** Per Art. 26.5, if the deployer cannot contact the provider, Art. 73 applies *mutatis mutandis* to the deployer
5. **Three exception categories have limited notification scope:** Annex III systems with equivalent EU notification obligations, medical devices (2017/745), and in vitro diagnostics (2017/746) -- these only need to report fundamental rights breaches
6. **Incomplete initial notification is permitted:** Art. 73.5 allows providers to submit a preliminary incomplete report to meet deadlines, followed by a complete notification
7. **Do not modify the AI system without informing authorities:** After notification, the provider must not alter the affected AI system in any way that could impact subsequent cause evaluation without first notifying competent authorities (Art. 73.6)
8. **AVMs must act within 7 days:** Market surveillance authorities have a maximum of 7 days to adopt adequate measures per the Market Surveillance Regulation after receiving notification
9. **Incident management must be embedded in the QMS:** Per Art. 17.1.i, serious incident notification procedures must be part of the provider's Quality Management System, not handled ad hoc
10. **Know your AI system's categorization:** Whether the system falls into an exception category determines the scope of notification obligations -- this must be documented
11. **Fundamental rights knowledge is mandatory:** Both provider and deployer must certify knowledge of EU fundamental rights to properly assess whether incidents require notification
12. **Multi-country notification is required:** If the AI system operates across multiple Member States, notification must be sent to the AVMs of all countries where the incident occurred

## 1. What is Technical Documentation for AI?

Technical documentation is the comprehensive set of information about a high-risk AI system that demonstrates compliance with Chapter III, Section 2 requirements. Per Recital (71) of the AI Act, it must include:

- **General characteristics, capabilities, and limitations** of the system
- **Algorithms, data, and training/testing/validation processes** used
- **Risk management system** documentation, clear and complete
- Must be kept **updated throughout the entire useful life** of the AI system

Technical documentation is a **significant part** of the conformity assessment process (Recital 173). The AI Act addresses two aspects: what the documentation must **contain** (Art. 11, Annex IV) and how it must be **conserved** (Art. 18).

---

## 2. Regulatory Articles

### Art. 11 - Technical Documentation

- **11.1:** Technical documentation shall be prepared **before** market introduction or putting into service, and kept **updated**. It shall demonstrate compliance with Chapter III, Section 2 requirements and provide authorities with clear and complete information to assess conformity. It shall contain, at minimum, the elements in **Annex IV**. SMEs and startups may provide simplified documentation using a Commission-established simplified form
- **11.2:** For HRAIS associated with products under Annex I, Section A harmonization legislation, a **single set of technical documents** shall be prepared containing all required information
- **11.3:** The Commission may adopt **delegated acts** to modify Annex IV to ensure documentation keeps pace with technical advances

### Art. 18 - Conservation of Documentation

- **18.1:** For a period of **10 years** from market introduction or putting into service, the provider shall keep the following available to national competent authorities:
  - a) Technical documentation (Art. 11)
  - b) Quality management system documentation (Art. 17)
  - c) Documentation on changes approved by notified bodies
  - d) Decisions and documents issued by notified bodies
  - e) EU declaration of conformity (Art. 47)
- **18.2:** Each Member State determines conditions for documentation availability during the 10-year period, including cases where a provider or authorized representative goes bankrupt or ceases activity
- **18.3:** Financial entity providers shall maintain technical documentation as part of documentation conserved under EU financial services law

| **IV.1** | General description of the AI system | 5.1 |

| **IV.3** | Foreseeable undesired results and risk sources | 5.4 |
| **IV.3** | Human oversight technical measures | 5.5 |
| **IV.3** | Input data specifications | 5.6 |
| **IV.4** | Performance parameter suitability | 5.7 |
| **IV.5** | Detailed risk management system description | 5.8 |
| **IV.6** | Pertinent changes throughout lifecycle | 5.9 |

| **IV.8** | EU declaration of conformity | 5.11 |
| **IV.9** | Post-market surveillance system | 5.12 |

## 3. Preparation of Documentation (Art. 11)

The provider is solely responsible for generating, updating, and conserving technical documentation. Key measures:

- **Plan** documentation scope from the design phase, including communication to involved departments/areas
- **Identify** applicable harmonized standards (CEN/CENELEC) and available standards (ISO/IEC, UNE CTN71 SC 42)
- **Explore** certification possibilities against existing standards
- Establish a **change tracking procedure** within the AI system's management processes
- Create a **chain of responsibility** -- designate a person/team responsible for updating documentation as changes occur
- **Define and dimension** a document management system or equivalent solution for conservation and access by notified bodies
- Documentation must be **complete before commercialization** and continuously updated afterward, especially after system updates

**SMEs and startups:** Must cover all the same aspects but may use simplified forms. Recommended to distribute documentation tasks to specific persons aligned with each guide's topics.

**Annex I, Section A products:** Technical documentation must be part of the existing documentation already generated for the product under its harmonized legislation.

## 4. Conservation of Documentation (Art. 18)

### Key Conservation Requirements

- **10-year retention period** from market introduction or putting into service
- Documentation must remain available to **national competent authorities** during this entire period
- The provider must select a **storage format** that guarantees documentation is not lost over 10 years and implement technical preservation measures
- Documentation must be available in the **format** established by national authorities
- The provider is **obligated to maintain documentation for the full 10 years even if the provider goes bankrupt or ceases activity**
- **Financial entities** must maintain technical documentation as part of their financial services regulatory documentation

### Five Categories of Documentation to Conserve

1. Technical documentation per Art. 11
2. Quality management system documentation per Art. 17
3. Documentation on changes approved by notified bodies
4. Decisions and other documents issued by notified bodies
5. EU declaration of conformity per Art. 47

## 5. Technical Documentation Structure (Annex IV)

### 5.1 General Description of the AI System (Annex IV.1)

Eight sub-elements required:

**(a) Intended purpose, provider name, system version:**
- Clear description of the use the system was designed for
- Context and conditions of use
- Provider information including human resource qualifications (roles across the AI lifecycle: data actors, configuration/operations, integration/MLOps, design/implementation, architecture/administration)
- Version control system with dates and relationship to previous versions

**(b) Hardware and software interactions:**
- Hardware: interoperability technology, data exchange formats, connection types, interconnection mechanisms (USB, WiFi, 5G, etc.), unique identification of input systems
- Software: integration format (input/output), interoperability mechanisms (API), communication channels, location relative to AI system, ownership (own service vs. SaaS)

**(c) Software and firmware versions:**
- Complete inventory of all software, firmware, libraries, third-party components
- For each: identification, version, incorporation date, update date
- Distinguish between AI components and non-AI algorithmic components
- Maintain version history table showing relationships between system versions

**(d) Forms of market introduction:**
- Commercialization modality: on-premise, IaaS, cloud (deployer's or provider's), SaaS, edge-computing
- For SaaS: API/interoperability details, commercial use levels, shared vs. dedicated hardware
- For installable formats: update policies, installation access, provider support
- For Annex I-A/II-A products: acquisition format, maintenance, product-AI integration details

**(e) Hardware description:**
- Computing requirements (processors, RAM, cache, storage) with minimums and recommended configurations
- Network characteristics (bandwidth, availability, latency)
- Secondary storage specifications
- Hardware recommendations for different usage configurations and data volumes

**(f) Product photographs/illustrations** (if AI is a product component):
- External characteristics showing the complete product
- CE marking location highlighted
- Internal layout identifying AI components vs. other accessories

**(g) Basic user interface description** provided to the deployer

**(h) Instructions of use** for the deployer, including:
- Clear indication it is an AI system
- How to interpret results with examples
- Human oversight level description and override mechanisms
- Precision metrics adapted for the deployer
- Transparency information (intended uses, non-recommended uses, performance, biases, data description)
- Training data configuration and validation data
- How to interpret log files and events
- Risk analysis information for the deployer (potential harm/benefits for demographic groups)
- Information about possible biases and their risks
- If a user interface exists: description of elements, relationship to intended purpose, accessibility, available languages

**Installation instructions** (for installable/configurable systems):
- Hardware and software requirements
- Environment configurations (production, development, testing)
- Step-by-step installation processes
- Applicable cybersecurity measures
- Log configuration implementation
- Log storage management for the deployer

### 5.2 Detailed Description of Elements and Development Process (Annex IV.2)

**(a) Development methodology:**
- AI approach/model selection process (supervised, unsupervised, etc.)
- Detailed model architecture description
- Software development steps and methodology (traditional, agile, MLOps)
- Training conditions and how precision/robustness metrics are achieved
- Model refinement process and experiments
- Experiment enumeration with validation criteria
- Model versioning criteria
- Data configuration summary and data versioning mechanism
- Training data typology, construction, and design considerations

**Pre-trained third-party systems:** Document model selection motivation, advantages, risk mitigation measures, version, and whether the provider developed it for HRAIS use

**(b) Design specifications:**
- **(b.1)** System logic, algorithm logic, key design decisions, assumptions about persons/groups who will use the system
- Link system specifications to intended purpose
- Select metrics per precision/robustness guides with motivation and ranges
- Define expected processing time
- Document assumptions about input data domains and design hypotheses
- If data involves persons, detail assumptions related to those groups
- Document assumptions related to human oversight design

- **(b.2)** Classification decisions: detailed categories and relationship to intended purpose; for non-classification systems, describe input/output ranges

- **(b.3)** Optimization objectives: model type (ML, statistical, etc.), architecture, model variant (supervised, reinforcement, deep learning), interaction diagram with all inputs/outputs, parameter descriptions with importance and optimization objectives

- **(b.4)** Expected outputs and quality: document any **trade-offs** made between requirements (e.g., increasing false positive rate to reduce risk of excluding eligible families from aid)

**(c) System architecture:**
- Component modeling using **C4 system** (high to low level) and **UML** for detailed architecture
- Server/equipment architecture, databases, network interconnection, container architectures
- Development, integration, and production environment descriptions
- For embedded/product AI systems: **emulation** of the final environment
- Complete software/hardware component inventory distinguishing AI vs. non-AI components

**(d) Data requirements:**
- **(d.1)** Training data: methodology, datasets with descriptive cards (purpose, provenance, scope, main characteristics), data formation methodology, internal labels, augmentation/noise techniques, data domains with ranges, data provenance
- **(d.2)** Data selection, labeling procedures, and cleansing methodologies: provenance registration, supervised learning labeling techniques, preprocessing/augmentation procedures, outlier handling

**(e) Human oversight evaluation:**
- Technical measures for output interpretation per Art. 14 and Art. 13.3(d)
- Functional description of measures, user experience details, visual/auditory mechanisms, transparency measures in system interface

**(f) Predetermined changes:**
- Date of change and system version
- Motivation description (change log)
- Record of AI system changes
- Self-contained update documentation referencing affected technical documentation sections
- Pre/post validation test result comparison (precision, computation costs, data volumes)

**(g) Validation and testing procedures:**
- **(g.1)** Validation procedures: unit test scope and code coverage, integration tests between components, interface/configuration testing methodology
- Validation/test data: data nature and variables, dataset dimensions and train/test/validation split, change registry over lifecycle, relationship between tests and model changes with precision metric values
- **(g.2)** Precision, robustness, and other Chapter III Section 2 metrics with ranges and motivation; cybersecurity documentation (plan, assets, vulnerabilities, controls for poisoning/adversarial/model-defect attacks)
- **(g.3)** Potentially discriminatory effects: discrimination analysis results with selected metrics (parity differences, disparate impact, outcome disparity, predictive feature differences, equality of opportunity, average probability differences); bias testing across sensitive categories
- **(g.4)** Test log records: date, data version, AI system version, results, responsible person signatures

**(h) Cybersecurity measures adopted** (covered in cybersecurity guide)

### 5.3 Monitoring, Functioning, and Control (Annex IV.3)

Documentation of the system's capabilities and limitations, including:
- Configurable parameter ranges, input/output data ranges
- Latency and efficiency measures for robustness
- Monitoring procedures for defined ranges
- Minimum acceptable values for robustness and precision metrics
- Notification mechanisms for system interruption/shutdown when guarantees cannot be met
- Known trade-offs (e.g., group fairness vs. individual privacy)
- Minimum expected guarantees and proven unattainable guarantees

### 5.4 Foreseeable Undesired Results and Risk Sources (Annex IV.3)

- Document **foreseeable but undesired outputs** -- results the system should not produce but may due to design characteristics (e.g., denying aid to an eligible family group because parameters fall outside training domain)
- Include summary of all risks identified in the risk management system related to health, fundamental rights, and discrimination

### 5.5 Human Oversight Measures (Annex IV.3)

- Precision description, typical failures, causes, and confidence intervals
- Elements considered for decision-making
- Simplified human oversight guides (what the system does, appropriate/inappropriate uses)
- **Examples** of cases where the system may take disparate decisions or fail
- Automation bias information and how to avoid it
- Explanatory video for the human oversight process with link/reference in documentation

### 5.6 Input Data Specifications (Annex IV.3)

- Expected input data format (text, images, sounds, time series, tabulated data)
- Reception frequency maintaining robustness and response time specifications
- Input data validations (security against inversion/extraction attacks, out-of-domain values)
- How input data is recorded in the system (accepted and rejected)

### 5.7 Performance Parameter Suitability (Annex IV.4)

Per the robustness guide, identify the most relevant performance parameters for the specific AI system and include a description of their suitability.

### 5.8 Risk Management System Description (Annex IV.5)

Include the risk management report and associated documentation of controls and applied measures as an integral part of technical documentation.

### 5.9 Change Control (Annex IV.6)

- Replicate the same information required in Section 5.2(f) for predetermined changes
- Each change must reference which technical documentation sections were adapted
- Maintain relationship between AI system version, date, and documentation version

### 5.10 Harmonized Standards or Equivalent Documentation (Annex IV.7)

- Inventory all harmonized standards used, with scope of application (published in EU Official Journal, issued by CEN/CENELEC)
- When harmonized standards are not applied, provide a detailed description of alternative solutions adopted to comply with Chapter III, Section 2, including other applicable standards (ISO, NIST)
- During the sandbox, since harmonized standards are not yet published, document the measures from each sandbox guide covering Chapter III, Section 2

### 5.11 EU Declaration of Conformity (Annex IV.8)

Per Art. 47, the declaration must be:
- In writing (machine-readable format, electronic or manuscript signature)
- Available to national competent authorities for **10 years**
- Specify the HRAIS for which it was issued

**Content per Annex V:**
1. Name and type of the AI system (unambiguous identification)
2. Provider name and address (plus authorized representative if applicable)
3. Statement that the conformity assessment is issued under the provider's exclusive responsibility
4. Affirmation of AI Act conformity (conformity assessment process must be completed)
5. Reference to harmonized standards and/or common specifications
6. Notified body identification (if involved in the conformity assessment process)
7. Place, date of issuance, name, and position of the signatory

For Annex II systems subject to other EU harmonization legislation, a **single declaration** shall be established containing the applicable harmonization legislation.

### 5.12 Post-Market Surveillance System (Annex IV.9)

- Document performance measurement technical measures and associated metrics throughout the system's production life
- Document the post-market surveillance plan per Art. 72
- Include solutions adopted for records conservation (Art. 12, 19) and relate stored information to the performance evaluation system
- The Commission will adopt an **implementing act** establishing a template for the surveillance plan by **2 February 2026**

## Key Points for Evaluating and Maintaining AI Projects

1. **Technical documentation must be complete before market introduction:** Art. 11.1 requires documentation to be prepared before the system enters the market or is put into service -- it cannot be done retroactively
2. **Annex IV defines the minimum content:** Nine sections covering general description, detailed elements/processes, monitoring/control, undesired results, human oversight, input data, performance parameters, risk management, changes, standards, conformity declaration, and post-market surveillance

5. **This guide integrates all other sandbox guides:** Technical documentation is the central repository where documentation from every other guide (risk management, precision, robustness, cybersecurity, human oversight, data governance, records, surveillance) must be incorporated
6. **Trade-offs must be explicitly documented:** When design decisions involve compromises between requirements (e.g., precision vs. safety), document the aspects involved, motivation, functional and technical descriptions, and consequences relative to the intended purpose
7. **Discriminatory effects analysis is mandatory:** Technical documentation must include discrimination analysis results using specific metrics (parity differences, disparate impact, outcome disparity) and testing across sensitive categories
8. **SMEs may use simplified documentation:** Art. 11.1 allows SMEs and startups to provide Annex IV elements in simplified form using a Commission-established simplified form, though the same topics must be covered
9. **Pre-trained and third-party models require specific documentation:** When using pre-trained models, document the selection motivation, advantages, risk mitigation measures, version, provider information, and any modifications made
10. **Instructions of use must be comprehensive and layered:** Must cover from the deployer (as an organization) down to the final user/operator, including human oversight procedures, risk information, bias warnings, and log interpretation guidance
11. **System architecture documentation should use C4 and UML:** The guide recommends C4 modeling for high-to-low-level component description and UML for detailed architecture, including development, integration, and production environments
12. **Test records must be signed and dated:** All validation test reports must include the test date, data version, AI system version, results, and signatures of responsible persons -- for both initial validation and post-update testing

|---|---|---|

### Diagnosis Phase (Phase II)

The diagnosis phase has **two objectives:**

1. **Self-diagnosis:** Entities assess their system against the AIA requirements using the measures proposed in the guides, identifying the **theoretical gap** remaining for compliance
2. **Adaptation Plan (PDA):** Design a work plan with the measures each entity will implement to achieve compliance, to be executed in a later phase

The checklist tool enables entities to:
- **Self-diagnose** compliance in a simple way, extracting **progress indicators** and **conclusions** about their system's status
- Design an **Adaptation Plan (PDA)** — the work plan for implementing measures to achieve AIA compliance

There is **one checklist per requirement** — 12 checklists total, one for each: QMS, risk management, human oversight, data governance, transparency, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, records, technical documentation, post-market surveillance, and serious incident management.

## 2. Introduction

The checklists have been **successfully tested** in the sandbox but are **not restricted** to sandbox participants. Any entity can use them to:

- Self-diagnose compliance of their high-risk AI systems against the AIA
- Design an adaptation plan for their systems

The tool is simple and intuitive, supported by the companion requirement guides that detail each proposed measure and the rationale for its selection.

## 3. Checklist Tool Structure

The checklist is an **Excel document** with the **same structure** across all 12 requirements. It has **9 tabs** — 5 informative (context and instructions) and 4 operative (where entities fill in their self-assessment data).

### Tab Order (recommended to follow sequentially)

| Tab | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 Cover Page | Informative | Confidentiality notice for SEDIA and sandbox participants |
| 3.2 Intro | Informative | Summary of steps and what the tool enables |
| 3.3 AIA Article | Informative | Article sections (subsections) being self-diagnosed, with summarised descriptions |
| 3.4 Guide Measures (GM) | Informative | Guide measures explained in detail with orientative questions |
| 3.5 GM-Subsection Mapping | Informative | Matrix showing which guide measures apply to which article subsections |
| 3.6 GM Self-Assessment | **Operative** | Self-evaluation of guide measures — maturity level and difficulty assessment |
| 3.7 Additional Measures (AM) | **Operative** | Entity-proposed additional measures beyond the guides |
| 3.8 AM-Subsection Mapping | **Operative** | Matrix mapping additional measures to article subsections |
| 3.9 AM Self-Assessment | **Operative** | Self-evaluation of additional measures — maturity and difficulty |

### Three-Step Workflow

1. **Review all article subsections** — self-evaluate the maturity level of guide measures (GM) that potentially help comply with the AIA
2. **Propose additional measures** — entities can contribute their own measures for each article subsection; these are evaluated by SEDIA and, if adequate, incorporated into the guides
3. **Map additional measures to article subsections** — indicate which article subsections the additional measures apply to, along with their maturity level

## 4. Tab Details

### 4.1 Cover Page [Informative]

Confidentiality reminder mandatory for SEDIA (Secretary of State for Digitalization and AI) and all sandbox participant entities regarding use and distribution.

### 4.2 Intro [Informative]

Summarised steps and what the tool enables. Entities may consult their **mentor** for advisory support at all steps.

### 4.3 AIA Article [Informative]

Displays the article subsections being self-diagnosed, with a "Summarised description" field for each subsection to facilitate contextual understanding in subsequent tabs.

### 4.4 Guide Measures (GM) [Informative]

Lists guide measures with full detail. Key characteristics:

- Derived from **best practices** in the software industry, **validated** by national and European entities
- Expected to be **more detailed** than the harmonised standards the European Commission will release (standards focus on WHAT to do; guides focus on HOW)
- **Not all measures apply** to every system — applicability depends on system characteristics
- **Orientative questions** accompany each measure to help contextualise whether the system already meets it

Entities may also propose **additional measures (AM)** they are already using that could also be valid for AIA compliance.

### 4.5 GM-Subsection Mapping [Informative]

A **cross-reference matrix** showing which guide measures (GM) potentially apply to which article subsections. Provides an executive visual summary of measure-to-article relationships.

### 4.6 GM Self-Assessment [Operative]

The **first operative tab**. Pre-populated with one row per measure-article relationship. For each row, two columns must be completed:

**Perceived Difficulty Level:**
| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 00. High | High difficulty |
| 01. Medium | Medium difficulty |
| 02. Low | Low difficulty |

**Maturity Level:**
| Value | Meaning | Adaptation Plan Action |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Not documented, not implemented | 01. Document and implement |
| L2 | Documentation in progress, not implemented | 01. Document and implement |
| L3 | Documented, not implemented | 02. Implement |
| L4 | Documented, implementation in progress | 02. Implement |
| L5 | Documented and implemented | 03. Full adaptation |
| L6 | Not documented but implemented | 04. Document |
| L7 | Documentation in progress and implemented | 04. Document |
| L8 | Measure not necessary for this system | 05. No adaptation required |

Selecting a maturity level **automatically** updates:
- **Diagnostic Status** column — changes from "00. Pending diagnostic" to "01. Already diagnosed" (tracks progress)
- **Adaptation Plan** column — sets the appropriate adaptation action based on the maturity-to-plan mapping above

### 4.7 Additional Measures (AM) [Operative]

Appears **blank** — entities add their own measures not covered by the guides. For each additional measure:

- **Brief description:** Identifying description (recommended to match GM naming convention)
- **File name:** Document where the measure is detailed; filling this automatically sets "Additional measure documented" to "01. Already contributed" (default: "00. Pending contribution")

Additional measures are **validated by SEDIA** (or the corresponding authority):

| SEDIA Evaluation Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 00. Pending | Awaiting SEDIA evaluation |
| 01. OK | SEDIA approved — a success, as it contributes a new measure applicable in Spain and Europe |
| 02. NO_OK | SEDIA rejected (after internal and external expert review) |

SEDIA also provides a **Comments** column for feedback on any of the three statuses.

### 4.8 AM-Subsection Mapping [Operative]

Maps additional measures from the previous tab to article subsections. Additional measure identifiers auto-populate in column headers. Mark with **X (uppercase)** which article subsection each measure applies to.

### 4.9 AM Self-Assessment [Operative]

Final step. Auto-populated with one row per additional-measure-to-article relationship. Same two-column input as GM Self-Assessment: **perceived difficulty level** and **maturity level**. Diagnostic Status and Adaptation Plan columns auto-update identically.

## Key Points for Evaluating and Maintaining AI Projects

1. **12 checklists cover all high-risk AI requirements:** One checklist per requirement area — QMS, risk management, human oversight, data governance, transparency, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, records, technical documentation, post-market surveillance, and serious incident management
2. **Self-diagnosis produces an Adaptation Plan:** The maturity assessment automatically generates a plan of action (document, implement, document and implement, full adaptation, or no adaptation needed) for each measure-article relationship
3. **Eight maturity levels (L1-L8) capture documentation and implementation status:** From "not documented, not implemented" (L1) through "documented and implemented" (L5) to "measure not necessary" (L8) — each maps to a specific adaptation action
4. **Three difficulty levels help prioritise:** High (00), Medium (01), and Low (02) perceived difficulty ratings help entities sequence their adaptation work
5. **Guide measures are not exhaustive:** Entities can propose additional measures (AM) based on their own experience; these are evaluated by SEDIA and, if validated, incorporated into the official guides
6. **The tool is not restricted to sandbox participants:** Any entity developing high-risk AI systems can use the checklists for self-assessment and adaptation planning
7. **Nine tabs follow a mandatory sequence:** Five informative tabs provide context (cover, intro, article subsections, guide measures, measure-article matrix), followed by four operative tabs for actual self-evaluation
8. **Automatic progress tracking:** Selecting a maturity level automatically updates diagnostic status and adaptation plan columns, providing real-time visibility into assessment progress
9. **Guide measures come from validated best practices:** Derived from software industry best practices and validated by national and European entities — they focus on HOW to comply (vs. harmonised standards which focus on WHAT)
10. **SEDIA validates additional measures:** Measures proposed by entities undergo SEDIA review (approved, rejected, or pending), with external expert consultation for rejections — creating a feedback loop that enriches the guides
11. **Cross-reference matrices provide executive visibility:** Both guide measures and additional measures have matrix tabs showing which article subsections each measure addresses, enabling gap analysis at a glance
12. **The Adaptation Plan feeds the next sandbox phase:** The diagnostic output (PDA) becomes the work plan executed in Phase III (Implementation), making this checklist the bridge between understanding requirements and achieving compliance
